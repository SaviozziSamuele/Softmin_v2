## Reply to MmRZ

We thank the reviewer for the positive comments and the precise questions.
About the weaknesses:
- **W1** For both our method and Langevin, we compute $n$ gradients and $n$ objective evaluations per step. The quantities that need to be computed specifically for the Softmin method are the Gibbs weights, which use values already evaluated, resulting in $O(n)$ extra scalar operations. We will report wall-clock times to show that computation times are comparable: on the transformer at depth 50, a Softmin step takes 2.5 s against 2.0 s for Adam (App. E.4.6).

- **W2** The interaction term crucially changes the sign of the gradient, as well as its magnitude. This lets suboptimal particles perform gradient ascent and leads to the barrier-reduction result.

About the questions:
- **Q1** The remark fails to make clear the comparison between Softmin and independent Langevin. In the remark we will state the expected exit time for independent Langevin, which in the small-noise regime satisfies $\lim_{\sigma\to0}\sigma\log\mathbb E\tau=h$. The $1/\beta$ factor results from the quasipotential computation and represents the action that a particle has to pay to go uphill out of the basin, until it reaches the point where the sign reverses (due to the interaction term) and it is pushed by the gradient up to the saddle point $s$. This computation (Theorem 3.4, parts 1–2) is what shows that the barrier $h$ is capped uniformly at order $1/\beta$ at the quasipotential level, while for independent Langevin it is exactly $h$.

- **Q2** The iteration budget is actually fixed, and the same for all methods. We let each method run with $100$ particles up to a limit of $10^7$ steps, with hyperparameters calibrated at the midpoint of the difficulty range (the x-axis). Figure 1 shows the success rate and the expected cost of each method. A run is considered failed when it does not reach the lower minimum within the limit; runs whose ensemble collapses or that stop improving for $5\cdot 10^5$ steps are stopped early and counted as failed. The expected running cost grows when a method loses reliability, which happens for all methods but Softmin as the potential barrier grows. This loss of reliability is what makes the Langevin method scale badly compared to Softmin. We will make all this more explicit in the main body, rather than leaving it to the appendix alone.

- **Q3** Thank you for pointing this out; we will cite Polyak (1963), Łojasiewicz (1963), and Karimi, Nutini & Schmidt (2016) in Definition 2.3.

## Reply to q1kz
We thank the reviewer for a careful reading of the theory. We agree with part of the criticism and want to be precise about what the paper claims.

- In the paper we limit our main theoretical result to the computation of the Freidlin–Wentzell quasipotential, which is shown to be capped at order $1/\beta$ uniformly in the barrier height. However, the reviewer is right in saying that the $\sigma\to0$ limit is not shown to be uniform in $h$, since it is taken at fixed $h$ and sub-exponential factors can depend on it. The main point is that the comparison is between exponents in $1/\sigma$ at fixed $h$: exactly $h$ for independent Langevin, and a quasipotential bound of order $1/\beta$ for Softmin. In the $\sigma\to0$ limit, the exponential factors are what determine the exit times, and these are correctly captured by the FW quasipotential. A prefactor of order $e^{\beta h}$ would not change these exponents, but it could matter at finite noise, and the paper does not control it; we will state this explicitly. On the double well, with $\beta h$ up to about $2{,}250$, Softmin reaches the target in every run within $10^5$ steps, so no growth of that order appears in practice.

Computing the exact exit time poses difficulties beyond the computation of the Eyring–Kramers prefactors, and the reviewer is right that this is not laid out in the paper.

On the specific step, we could not find an approximation $r\ll e^{\beta r}$ in Lemma 3.3 or its proof. The cap follows from the exact inequality
$$u(r)=\frac{n\,r\,e^{-\beta r}}{n-1+e^{-\beta r}}<\frac{n}{n-1}\,r\,e^{-\beta r}\le\frac{n}{e(n-1)\beta}\qquad(r>0).$$
If the reviewer had another line in mind, we would be grateful for the pointer.

- About Corollary 3.5, the reviewer is right: it only concerns the exit from the initial basin $D_-$, and it cannot describe the jump beyond $\xi_+$, since $\Gamma(s)$ is itself a local minimum of $nJ_\beta$ when $r_c<h$. For this second crossing the paper only gives a quasipotential bound, $u_c-u(h)<u_c$ (Theorem 3.4, part 3). Moreover, the boundary of $D_-$ contains the point where the sign of the pre-factor flips, which is a saddle of $nJ_\beta$, so the exit theorem recalled in Appendix B does not apply as stated; we will restate the corollary with a theorem that allows a characteristic boundary. Corollary 3.5 alone is therefore not enough to make a statement about exit times, and we do not make an unconditional one in the paper. This should be made clear in a remark following the corollary, and we thank the reviewer for pointing it out.

- We are sorry that the reviewer found the presentation of the paper insufficient. We will take this into account and improve the readability of both the main body and the appendix.

- About the transformer experiment: as the reviewer points out, it is not meant to further support reliability, but to show that our method scales to very high dimensions. We need to make this scope clearer, and we thank the reviewer for this comment. As for the x-axis, Adam and SGD also run as 10 independent chains, so every method performs the same 10 forward and backward passes per step; we will nonetheless plot gradient evaluations and wall-clock time instead of steps.

## Reply to nDsh

We thank the reviewer for the detailed review. We checked the calculations in it and agree with most of them.

- **W1/Q1** The reviewer is right that $\beta$ and $T_0$ were selected under different criteria. The escape time of Simulated Annealing decreases monotonically in $T_0$, so selecting it by escape time would pick the largest value in any grid; we excluded this with the Arrhenius criterion, but we agree that this does not justify a different objective. We have not re-run Simulated Annealing during the discussion, so we cannot say whether the high-barrier advantage survives: under geometric cooling a larger $T_0$ should shift the barrier at which it freezes rather than remove it, but this has to be checked. In a revised version we will select $T_0$ and the cooling rate jointly by ERT at the same point used for $\beta$, without the Arrhenius restriction, extend the sweep until the schedule fails, and report the outcome either way. Until then, our conclusions on Simulated Annealing are conditional on the calibrated schedule.

- **W2/Q2** The reviewer's calculation is correct: the $O(1/\beta)$ cap comes from the Gibbs weighting, which log-sum-exp shares, and we will say so. What the sign change adds is a smaller constant. From $q(r_c)=0$ one gets exactly $u_c=n(r_c-1/\beta)$, so $u_c/v_\infty=(\beta r_c-1)/\log\frac{n}{n-1}$, which is $0.40$ at $n=2$ and decreases to $1/e$. The post-saddle structure also changes: for $h>r_c$, $J_\beta$ creates a shallow well at $\Gamma(s)$, but even adding this second crossing the channel cost $2u_c-u(h)$ stays below $v(h)$ for every $n\ge2$. The gain is therefore a constant factor in the bound on the exponent, not a different order in $\beta$. Empirically, only the $\beta=2$ ablation (App. E.5.4) shows the sign mattering; we will add the drift $-nw_k\nabla f(x_k)$ and a pure-noise control on the wells, with the same seeds, ensemble size, noise and budget.

- **Q3** The reviewer is right: the point $\Gamma(x_m)$ where the pre-factor flips is an index-one saddle of $nJ_\beta$ on $\partial D_-$ (for $h>r_c$ the Hessian there is diagonal, with entries $-n\beta w_1f'(x_m)^2<0$ and $\frac{n}{n-1}f''(z_-)>0$), so the exit theorem recalled in Appendix B does not apply as stated. We will restate Corollary 3.5 with an exit theorem that allows a characteristic boundary, restricted to the exit from $D_-$; we are still checking which result fits our setting and what its exact hypotheses are. For a gradient drift, such a theorem gives $\lim_{\sigma\to0}\sigma\log\mathbb E\tau_{D_-}=\min_{\partial D_-}nJ_\beta-nJ_\beta(\Gamma(z_-))\le u_h$. For our double wells, $\Gamma(z_-)$ and $\Gamma(x_m)$ are nondegenerate, while the boundedness of $D_-$ and the equilibria on its boundary remain to be checked. The quasipotential bounds of Theorem 3.4 are not affected.

- **Q4** Yes, unsuccessful runs are stopped early, for every method, when the ensemble collapses (largest per-coordinate standard deviation below $10^{-6}$) or the best value does not improve for $5\cdot10^5$ steps. All failed Simulated Annealing runs stop by collapse at about $5.1\cdot10^5$ steps, which gives the value in Table 12 ($0.275\times100\times512{,}900\approx1.41\times10^7$), and all failed Langevin runs stop by stagnation at $5.49\cdot10^5$ steps. The caption of Table 12 is wrong: a failed run contributes the steps it ran, not the full budget. We will fix it and describe the rule in App. E.3. Since failed runs are charged less than the full budget, the cost ratios are conservative. Stopping does not change the outcomes: the collapsed Simulated Annealing ensemble is frozen, and for Langevin at the smallest failing barrier Kramers' formula gives about $10^8$ time units for the first of 100 particles to escape, against a full budget of $10^4$.
