\begin{abstract}
    Non-convex optimization in high dimensions requires balancing gradient information with global exploration, a tension that Langevin dynamics and other gradient-informed methods address only partially. We introduce a gradient-based swarm method driven by a Softmin energy interaction $J_\beta(\xx)$, a smooth approximation of the minimum over the ensemble that couples particles through their relative energies, and add Brownian noise to its gradient flow. We show that at every stable equilibrium the interaction splits the ensemble into particles at local minima and particles at local maxima; that without noise the ensemble's best value converges at least as fast as gradient flow under a Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition; and that the interaction caps the Freidlin--Wentzell quasipotential barrier between adjacent basins at order $1/\beta$, independently of the true barrier height and strictly below the barrier an overdamped Langevin particle faces. Consistently with this cap, on double-well and quadruple-well benchmarks Softmin reaches the target in every run at every barrier height tested, while Simulated Annealing, Langevin dynamics, and variants of Particle Swarm Optimization and Consensus-Based Optimization all eventually fail; counting failed runs, it is more than an order of magnitude cheaper than Simulated Annealing overall, though not on low barriers. Nor is this a mere effect of ensemble size: Softmin succeeds in every run with five particles, whereas Simulated Annealing gains from more particles only what independent restarts predict, enough on moderate barriers but not on high ones. On Lennard-Jones clusters, Softmin's energy gap to the best known minimum stays nearly flat as the cluster grows while every baseline's rises, and the method trains transformers with millions of parameters. Its limits are equally clear: Particle Swarm Optimization is faster on a high-dimensional Ackley function, and Adam on transformer training, where no method struggled.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Non-convex optimization problems are notoriously difficult, as the presence of multiple local minima can trap gradient-based algorithms. Existing approaches such as gradient descent perform well for convex functions with a single minimum but will likely converge to suboptimal local minima in more complex landscapes. This challenge is a central motivation in machine learning more broadly. For example, non-convex losses in deep neural network training, and sharp local minima in reinforcement learning objectives, are two prominent settings where escaping suboptimal basins is a recognized bottleneck. Most of the benchmarks in this paper (Section~\ref{sec:numerical-experiments}) are analytically tractable landscapes on which the height of the barriers can be controlled exactly, but we also train a small transformer (Section~\ref{sec:transformer}) to test the method on a neural-network loss.

A classical strategy to address this issue is to add a noise term to the dynamics, allowing the system to escape local minima and leading to stochastic gradient-based methods, such as Langevin dynamics \cite{CoffeyKalmykovWaldron2004} and noise-perturbed stochastic gradient descent \cite{fehrman2020convergence}. These methods remain effective but typically exhibit slow transition times between local minima, since the mean escape time of a particle subject to Gaussian noise grows exponentially in the height of the surrounding potential barrier \cite{berglund2011kramers}. Methods that improve on this convergence rate often rely on additional structural assumptions on the objective $f$, for instance a decomposition into simpler component functions amenable to specialized solvers \cite{xu2018global}. In more general settings where such structure is not available, Simulated Annealing and its variants \cite{kirkpatrick1983optimization,gelfand1990sampling,tang2024discrete} are widely used, leveraging a decaying temperature schedule to shift the dynamics from noisy exploration to refined local search.

A natural extension of stochastic gradient-based approaches is the use of multiple particles to explore the search space in parallel. For instance, when particles evolve independently under Langevin dynamics, the expected transition times between local minima decrease compared to single-particle dynamics purely due to statistical averaging over multiple independent trajectories. Moreover, allowing particles to cooperate rather than evolve independently, as in Interacting Particle Langevin Dynamics \cite{akyildiz2025interacting,encinar2025proximal}, can further improve exploration and convergence. The idea of particle cooperation is often central in Metaheuristic methods \cite{bandaru2016metaheuristic,yang2020nature}. Such methods provide a broad framework for global optimization and have demonstrated strong empirical performance, though they largely lack the rigorous theoretical convergence guarantees available for gradient-based methods \cite{hussain2019metaheuristic,rajwar2023exhaustive}. Many such methods employ a particle-swarm-like paradigm \cite{hussain2019metaheuristic,rajwar2023exhaustive}, in which multiple interacting particles explore the search space in parallel to locate global minimizers.

The global optimization method proposed in this paper builds on these ideas by combining stochastic gradient techniques with cooperative multi-particle dynamics. We employ the well-known Softmin energy functional $J_\beta$, which smoothly interpolates between the average and the hard minimum of the function values across particle positions. This functional acts as the source of interaction among particles, whose evolution is governed by its gradient, driving the system toward more effective exploration and optimization.

The Softmin function provides a natural way to interpolate between uniform weighting and hard minima via inverse temperature $\beta>0$. A common use of the Softmin function is to define the Gibbs distribution, where the probability of sampling index $i$ out of values $v_1,\dots,v_n$ is given by:
\[
    \mathbb{P}[i] = \tfrac{\exp{(-\beta{v_i})}}{\sum_{j=1}^n \exp{(-\beta v_j})},
\]
with $\beta\to\infty$ concentrating on $\min_{i} v_i$ and $\beta\to0$ uniform \cite{bertsekas1999nonlinear}. This underlies Boltzmann-machine learning \cite{ackley1985boltzmann, srivastava2012multimodal}, sampling and annealing methods for stochastic optimization \cite{gelfand1990sampling, gelfand2000gibbs}, and exploration in Reinforcement Learning \cite{pan2019reinforcement, haarnoja2017reinforcement}.

Our proposed method is a stochastic gradient-based particle swarm approach that leverages a Softmin-type energy functional to effectively incorporate gradient information from the particles. Consider a differentiable function $f:\R^d\to\R$. The objective is to approximately solve the global minimization problem $\min_{x\in\R^d}f(x)$. Given a population of $n$ particles $\xx=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ with $x_i\in\R^d$ for all $i\in[n]$, the Softmin energy is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    J_\beta(\xx)=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\exp(-\beta f(x_i))}{\sum_{j=1}^n\exp(-\beta f(x_j))}f(x_i),
    \label{eq:Softmin-energy}
\end{equation}
which provides a smooth approximation of the empirical minimum among the $f(x_i)$. The parameter $\beta>0$ controls the approximation's sharpness: larger values yield a closer approximation to the hard minimum $\min_i f(x_i)$, while $\beta\to0$ recovers the average $\frac 1 n \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i)$.

To minimize $J_\beta$ we consider the stochastic gradient flow in the particle space $\R^{nd}$:
\begin{equation}\label{e:sde}
   \dd\xx_t = -n\nabla J_{\beta_t}(\xx_t)\dd t+\sqrt{2\sigma}\dd B_t,
\end{equation}
where $(B_t)_{t\geq 0}$ represents a standard $nd$-dimensional Brownian motion and $\sigma>0$ introduces stochasticity to promote exploration and escape from local minima. The inverse temperature $\beta_t$ may vary over time: the ensemble-descent guarantee of Theorem~\ref{thm:ensemble-min} holds for an arbitrary such schedule, but the barrier-flattening analysis of Section~\ref{sec:hitting-times} fixes $\beta$ to a constant, and Remark~\ref{rem:higher-dim} reports that, empirically, a fixed, well-tuned $\beta$ outperforms annealing it.

\subsection{Contribution}
The main novelty of this work is the rigorous analysis of stochastic gradient-based dynamics driven by the Softmin energy functional, $J_\beta(\xx)$. In this paper, we prove that the resulting inter-particle interaction mechanism provides two concrete theoretical guarantees, detailed below: a no-slowdown guarantee on the ensemble's best objective value, and a provable cap on the effective escape barrier between basins. We then show empirically where the method is reliable and where it is not.

\paragraph{Dynamic Ensemble Partitioning and No-Slowdown Convergence} The interaction induced by $\nabla J_\beta$ naturally partitions the particle ensemble via an effective learning rate, $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$. At every stable equilibrium, particles below the Softmin threshold ($\prefactor{k}{\xx} > 0$) sit at local minima, while trapped, high-energy particles ($\prefactor{k}{\xx} < 0$) are automatically repelled to hill-climb and explore, with at least one particle always at a local minimum (Theorem~\ref{thm:stationary-points}). This sign is not fixed to any single particle over time, so no individual trajectory is permanently assigned the minimizing role; nonetheless, the ensemble's running best objective value is always nonincreasing, and, whenever $f$ satisfies a Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition on the initial sublevel set, it converges to the global minimum at least as fast as ordinary gradient flow, for any inverse-temperature schedule (Theorem~\ref{thm:ensemble-min}).

\paragraph{Provable Barrier Flattening} We demonstrate that the Softmin dynamics do not merely reduce effective potential barriers, but analytically cap them. By integrating the effective drift along an explicit escape channel, we prove that arbitrarily high physical barriers separating adjacent basins are capped, at the level of the Freidlin--Wentzell quasipotential, to a constant of order $1/\beta$, uniformly in the true barrier height (Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time}). This bound holds unconditionally on the quasipotential itself, strictly below the exact small-noise exit exponent of an overdamped Langevin particle in the same landscape; turning it into an exponentially cheaper guarantee on the expected transition time additionally requires the metastable-domain hypotheses made precise in Corollary~\ref{cor:exit-time-conditional}. This structural landscape modulation is a direct consequence of the continuous Softmin repulsion, distinguishing our approach from generic stochastic swarm methods and Simulated Annealing.

\paragraph{Reliability Beyond Independent Restarts} On benchmarks whose difficulty is set by the height of a barrier, Softmin reaches the target in every run at every difficulty tested, where every baseline eventually fails; counting failed runs, this makes it more than an order of magnitude cheaper than Simulated Annealing overall, although Simulated Annealing is cheaper on low barriers. Varying the ensemble size shows that this reliability is not simply a benefit of running many particles, which would help even independent ones through statistical averaging: Softmin succeeds in every run with as few as five particles, whereas Simulated Annealing gains from additional particles just what independent restarts predict, enough on moderate barriers but not on high ones. The method also carries over to higher dimension: on Lennard-Jones clusters its energy gap to the best known minimum stays nearly flat as the cluster grows while every baseline's grows, and it trains transformers with millions of parameters. We report the limits as prominently. Particle Swarm Optimization is faster on a high-dimensional Ackley function, and Adam is faster at transformer training, where no method had difficulty reaching the target. Throughout, we present agreement between these results and the theory as consistency rather than confirmation, since the analytic benchmarks run at a noise level at which the proven barrier cap is small.

\subsection{Related work}

\paragraph{Metaheuristic optimization methods}
Metaheuristic optimization methods include Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO) \cite{CarrilloChoiTotzeckTse2018,fornasier2024consensus,pinnau2017consensus, carrillo2021consensus}, Genetic Algorithms \cite{Goldberg1989}, Ant Colony Optimization \cite{DorigoStutzle2004}, and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) \cite{kennedy1995particle, noel2012new}. Traditional particle-swarm approaches typically operate without explicit gradient information, relying instead on position-based updates to coordinate particles toward promising regions. When reliable gradient information is available, leveraging it enables convergence rates comparable to standard stochastic gradient descent, which gradient-agnostic metaheuristics cannot achieve.

Recent gradient-based swarm methods \cite{riedl2023gradient, bolte2024swarm, carrillo2021consensus, noel2012new} have made progress by incorporating gradient information into multi-particle dynamics. However, our approach differs fundamentally in its mechanism for inducing exploration and escaping barriers. Rather than consensus-based averaging or general gradient-weighted potentials, we employ the Softmin energy functional to explicitly create a minimizing/maximizing partition of the particle ensemble: particles below the Softmin threshold are drawn toward local minima via gradient descent, while particles above the threshold are repelled toward maxima. This automatic role assignment (Theorem~\ref{thm:stationary-points}) simultaneously reduces effective potential barriers (Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time}) and forces suboptimal particles into exploratory regimes without requiring manual basin-switching heuristics.

To the best of our knowledge, no other metaheuristic employs the Softmin energy functional, computed as the Softmin of function values at particle positions, as the sole source of inter-particle interaction. This design enables provable escape from suboptimal local minima with a barrier bounded independently of the true barrier height (Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time}, Section~\ref{sec:hitting-times}), together with a deterministic guarantee that the ensemble's best value never lags ordinary gradient flow under a Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition (Theorem~\ref{thm:ensemble-min}, Section~\ref{sec:convergence-rate}). Section~\ref{sec:numerical-experiments} compares these predictions with experiments, reporting both where the observed behavior is consistent with the mechanism and where it is not.

\paragraph{Langevin-based sampling and interacting-particle methods}
Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD) \cite{welling2011bayesian} and its non-asymptotic analysis for non-convex optimization \cite{raginsky2017nonconvex} study a single diffusion whose drift is the gradient of the objective itself, with guarantees governed by the spectral gap of the associated Gibbs measure; when multiple particles are used, interaction typically enters only through averaging over independent runs. Interacting Particle Langevin Dynamics (IPLD) \cite{akyildiz2025interacting, encinar2025proximal} instead couples an ensemble of particles through their empirical measure, using this mean-field interaction to build unbiased or stabilized estimators of the marginal likelihood gradient in latent-variable models. Our construction is closer in spirit to IPLD, in that it also relies on genuine ensemble-level coupling rather than independent copies, but the interaction itself is different in kind: instead of pooling particles through an empirical average, the Softmin energy $J_\beta$ couples particles through their relative energies, producing a state-dependent, sign-changing effective learning rate $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$ that deterministically assigns each particle a minimizing or maximizing role (Theorem~\ref{thm:stationary-points}). It is this competitive, temperature-sharpened coupling, rather than mean-field averaging, that produces the barrier-capping mechanism of Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time}, and it is aimed directly at global optimization of $f$ rather than at marginal-likelihood estimation.

\paragraph{Connection to Statistical Physics and Functional Design}
Our approach shares conceptual roots with population-based statistical physics methods such as Population Annealing (PA) \cite{Wang_2015}, which also employs a Gibbs-weighted ensemble to locate global ground states. However, while PA relies on discrete demographic selection, duplicating low-energy particles and eliminating high-energy ones prior to local Markov Chain Monte Carlo steps, our method maintains a fixed population size and drives exploration through continuous dynamical repulsion. This active exploration mechanism differentiates our Softmin energy functional from the standard log-sum-exp (or free-energy) functional, $F_\beta(\xx) = -{\beta}^{-1} \log \sum e^{-\beta f(x_i)}$. The gradient of the log-sum-exp formulation, $\nabla_{x_k} F_\beta(\xx) = w_k \nabla f(x_k)$, strictly preserves gradient descent, causing sub-optimal particles with small weights to simply stagnate. In contrast, the gradient of our proposed Softmin functional, $J_\beta(\xx) = \sum w_i f(x_i)$, natively incorporates the sign-changing effective learning rate, $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$. Consequently, rather than discarding trapped particles, the geometry of the Softmin functional actively destabilizes their local minima, forcing high-energy particles to hill-climb and escape via the energy barrier-flattening mechanism formalized in our theoretical analysis.

\paragraph{Softmin energy} Theoretical work on the Gibbs Softmin (or Boltzmann Softmin) has primarily focused on its properties as a smooth approximation to the minimum operator \cite{bertsekas1999nonlinear}, convergence of softmax policy gradients in reinforcement learning \cite{mei2020global}, and its role in exploration–exploitation trade-offs via temperature scheduling \cite{song2019revisiting}. To our knowledge, this is the first work employing $J_\beta$ as an optimization objective for non-convex global search. This is conceptually distinct from swarm-gradient methods that anneal a mean-field particle density toward the Dirac mass at the global minimizer \cite{bolte2024swarm}: rather than acting on a population density, the Softmin energy couples particles directly through the relative ranking of their objective values. The effective barrier reduction via particle cooperation, analyzed in Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time}, is previously unstudied.

\subsection{Structure of the paper}
In Section~\ref{sec:gradient-flow-dynamics}, we analyze the deterministic gradient flow dynamics obtained by setting $\sigma =0$ in \eqref{e:sde} in detail. Section~\ref{sec:stationary-points} characterizes the stable equilibria of this flow with no convexity assumption on $f$: every particle sits at a critical point of $f$, the sign of its effective learning rate fixes whether that critical point is a minimum or a maximum, and at least one particle always resides at a local minimum. Section~\ref{sec:convergence-rate} then shows that, whenever $f$ satisfies a Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition, the best objective value across the ensemble decreases at least as fast as ordinary gradient flow, for any inverse-temperature schedule.

In Section~\ref{sec:hitting-times}, we examine the noisy dynamics \eqref{e:sde} at fixed inverse temperature, showing that the Softmin interaction caps the Freidlin--Wentzell quasipotential barrier separating adjacent basins at a level of order $1/\beta$, independently of the true barrier height -- strictly below the exact small-noise exit exponent of an overdamped Langevin particle in the same landscape.

In Section~\ref{sec:numerical-experiments}, we compare the method against seven baselines -- Simulated Annealing, overdamped Langevin dynamics, Gradient-Aware CBO \cite{carrillo2021consensus} with and without annealing, Gradient-Enhanced PSO \cite{noel2012new}, and the canonical gradient-free forms of both -- on double-well and quadruple-well landscapes with controlled barrier heights, Lennard-Jones clusters, and a high-dimensional Ackley function, and on the training of a transformer, where Adam and SGD are added as baselines. We also vary the ensemble size, to separate the effect of the Softmin coupling from that of running many particles. The experiments show better reliability once potential barriers exceed a critical threshold, together with two clear limits: a landscape whose difficulty is carried by the density of local minima rather than by their depth, and a neural-network training problem on which every method succeeds and Adam is fastest.

\section{Gradient Flow dynamics for Softmin energy}
\label{sec:gradient-flow-dynamics}

This section focuses on the deterministic gradient flow dynamics
\begin{equation}
    \dd\xx_t = -n\nabla J_\beta(\xx_t)\dd t,
    \label{eq:gradient-flow}
\end{equation}
where $\xx_t = (x_1(t), \dots, x_n(t))$ denotes the state of the particle system and the Softmin energy $J_\beta(\xx)$ is defined in \eqref{eq:Softmin-energy}. The scaling factor $n$ is fixed by the requirement that the dynamics reduce, in the high-temperature limit $\beta\to0$, to $n$ independent gradient particles: as shown below, every Gibbs weight $w_k\to1/n$ and every pre-factor $\prefactor{k}{\xx}\to1$ (defined precisely in \eqref{eq:softmin-gradient} below) in this limit, so \eqref{eq:gradient-flow} recovers $\dot x_k=-\nabla f(x_k)$ componentwise. In the same limit $\beta\to0$, it also keeps the per-particle drift of order one as $n$ grows, since then $w_k\to1/n$; we do not derive a mean-field limit in this paper. This continuous-time system performs gradient descent on the Softmin landscape, which is formulated such that its minimization inherently drives the ensemble toward the minima of the objective function $f$.

Subsequent analysis characterizes the stable equilibria of this flow without any convexity assumption on $f$: at every such equilibrium, each particle sits at a critical point of $f$, the sign of its effective learning rate $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}$ fixes whether that critical point is a minimum or a maximum, and at least one particle resides at a local minimum. Separately, we show that the ensemble's running best objective value is always nonincreasing, and decreases at least as fast as ordinary gradient flow toward the global minimum whenever $f$ satisfies a Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition, for any inverse temperature schedule.

To formally analyze these dynamics, it is necessary to first evaluate the gradient of $nJ_\beta$ with respect to the individual particle coordinates. Let $w_k = e^{-\beta f(x_k)}(\sum_j e^{-\beta f(x_j)})^{-1}$ denote the Gibbs weight of particle $x_k$, and define the dynamic pre-factor $\prefactor{k}{\xx} = nw_k\left[1 - \beta(f(x_k) - J_\beta(\xx))\right]$. The gradient of $nJ_\beta(\xx)$ with respect to particle $x_k$ is computed in Lemma~\ref{lem:derivative-of-J-proof} and is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{x_k} (nJ_\beta(\xx)) =\prefactor{k}{\xx} \nabla f(x_k),
    \label{eq:softmin-gradient}
\end{equation}
so that \eqref{eq:gradient-flow} reads componentwise as $\dot x_k=-\prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla f(x_k)$. The exact second-order partial derivatives and corresponding Hessian blocks, which are required for the subsequent local stability analysis, are collected in Lemma~\ref{lem:derivative-of-J-proof} in Appendix~\ref{apx:technical-proofs}.

This formulation reveals a critical property of the Softmin gradient. The dynamic pre-factor $\prefactor{k}{\xx} = nw_k\beta\left[1/\beta - f(x_k) + J_\beta(\xx)\right]$ dictates the effective direction of the gradient flow for each particle $x_k$, acting as a state-dependent learning rate. According to the dynamics in \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}, if a particle evaluates to a landscape value $f(x_k) < J_\beta(\xx) + 1/\beta$, the pre-factor $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$ is strictly positive, and the particle performs standard gradient descent on $f$; the particle is then said to be \textit{minimizing}. Conversely, if $f(x_k) > J_\beta(\xx) + 1/\beta$, the pre-factor becomes negative, effectively reversing the flow, and the particle is \textit{maximizing}, executing gradient ascent on $f$. Consequently, a particle is pushed uphill exactly when its value exceeds the ensemble threshold $J_\beta(\xx)+1/\beta$. A particle trapped in a sub-optimal local minimum is therefore repelled from it only once the rest of the ensemble has reached sufficiently lower values to place it above that threshold; a trapped particle below the threshold keeps minimizing $f$, as do all particles at comparatively lower energy states. Throughout, we refer to $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$ as the pre-factor, and use the term \emph{effective learning rate} specifically when emphasizing this interpretation of it as a state-dependent scaling of the gradient step.

Two elementary facts drive the equilibrium analysis that follows. First, summing the pre-factor over all particles and using $\sum_k w_k=1$ and $\sum_k w_k f(x_k)=J_\beta(\xx)$,
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{k=1}^n \prefactor{k}{\xx} = n \qquad \text{for every } \xx\in\R^{nd},
    \label{eq:sum-pre-factor}
\end{equation}
so at least one coefficient is always strictly positive. Second, at any configuration where every particle sits at a critical point of $f$, the mixed second partial derivatives of $J_\beta$ vanish identically, since each mixed Hessian block is an outer product carrying a factor $\nabla f(x_k)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:derivative-of-J-proof}); the Hessian of $nJ_\beta$ is then block diagonal.

\subsection{Stationary points of the dynamics}
\label{sec:stationary-points}

This subsection characterizes the stable equilibria of the gradient flow \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}. Theorem~\ref{thm:stationary-points} shows that at any such equilibrium every particle sits at a critical point of $f$ with a strictly nonzero pre-factor, the sign of that pre-factor fixes whether the critical point is a minimum or a maximum, and at least one particle always resides at a local minimum. Consequently, at every stable equilibrium at least one particle sits at a local minimum of $f$, and no particle sits at a strict local minimum whose value exceeds the threshold $J_\beta(\bar\xx)+1/\beta$, since such a particle would have a negative pre-factor and hence be at a local maximum; none of this requires a local geometric hypothesis on $f$.

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:stationary-points}
    Let $f\in C^2(\R^d)$, let $\beta>0$, and let $\bar\xx=(\bar x_1,\dots,\bar x_n)$ be a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium of \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}. Then, for every $k\in\{1,\dots,n\}$, evaluated at $\bar\xx$:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\nabla f(\bar x_k)=0$;
        \item $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}\ne0$;
        \item the sign of $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}$ fixes the type of $\bar x_k$:
        \[
            \begin{aligned}
                \prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}>0 &\implies \bar x_k \text{ is a local minimum of } f,\\
                \prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}<0 &\implies \bar x_k \text{ is a local maximum of } f,
            \end{aligned}
        \]
        with $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}>0 \iff f(\bar x_k)<J_\beta(\bar\xx)+1/\beta$.
    \end{enumerate}
    Moreover, by \eqref{eq:sum-pre-factor}, at least one particle sits at a local minimum of $f$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent The rigorous derivation of these stability conditions is given in Appendix~\ref{apx:technical-proofs}.

\begin{corollary}[Sufficient condition for stability]
\label{cor:stability-sufficient}
Let $\bar\xx=(\bar x_1,\dots,\bar x_n)$ satisfy $\nabla f(\bar x_k)=0$ for every $k$, and assume that for each $k$ either
\[
    \prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}>0 \text{ and } \nabla^2f(\bar x_k)\succ0,
    \qquad\text{or}\qquad
    \prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}<0 \text{ and } \nabla^2f(\bar x_k)\prec0,
\]
evaluated at $\bar\xx$. Then $\nabla^2(nJ_\beta)(\bar\xx)\succ0$; in particular $\bar\xx$ is a strict local minimum of $nJ_\beta$ and a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium of \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    By Lemma~\ref{lem:derivative-of-J-proof} the Hessian at a fully critical configuration is block diagonal with blocks $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}\nabla^2f(\bar x_k)$, and each hypothesis makes the corresponding block positive definite.
\end{proof}

By Theorem~\ref{thm:stationary-points} and Corollary~\ref{cor:stability-sufficient}, the deterministic flow admits stable equilibria at which particles sit at local minima and local maxima of $f$ simultaneously, not only at full consensus on a single minimum; which equilibrium is reached, and hence whether it is globally optimal, depends on the initialization, and the deterministic dynamics offer no guarantee of escaping a suboptimal one. This motivates injecting noise: the noisy dynamics analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:hitting-times} provide a mechanism for the ensemble to escape such traps.

\subsection{Descent of the ensemble minimum}
\label{sec:convergence-rate}

The instantaneous sign of the pre-factor $\prefactor{k}{\xx(t)}$ is not conserved for a fixed particle, since it depends on every coordinate through $J_\beta$; we therefore track instead the running best value across the ensemble,
\[
    m(t):=\min_{1\le k\le n}f(x_k(t)),
\]
controlling it for a general, possibly time-dependent, inverse-temperature schedule $\beta_t>0$, where $\prefactor{k}{\xx(t)}$ denotes the pre-factor evaluated at inverse temperature $\beta_t$ and configuration $\xx(t)$.

\begin{definition}[Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition]
    \label{def:pl-condition}
    A differentiable function $f:\R^d\to\R$ satisfies the \textit{Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz (PL) condition} on a set $S\subseteq\R^d$ with constant $\mu>0$ if
    \[
        \tfrac12\|\nabla f(x)\|^2\ge\mu\bigl(f(x)-f_\star\bigr), \qquad f_\star:=\inf f,
    \]
    for every $x\in S$.
\end{definition}


\begin{theorem}[Descent of the ensemble minimum]
    \label{thm:ensemble-min}
    Let $f\in C^1(\R^d)$ have locally Lipschitz gradient, let $\beta_t>0$ be measurable, and let $\xx(t)=(x_1(t),\dots,x_n(t))$ be a global solution of
    \[
        \dot x_k(t)=-\prefactor{k}{\xx(t)}\,\nabla f(x_k(t)), \qquad k=1,\dots,n.
    \]
    Suppose $f$ satisfies the Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition (Definition~\ref{def:pl-condition}) with constant $\mu>0$ on the sublevel set $S_0:=\{x:f(x)\le m(0)\}$. Then $m$ is nonincreasing and
    \[
        m(t)-f_\star\le e^{-2\mu t}\bigl(m(0)-f_\star\bigr), \qquad t\ge0.
    \]
\end{theorem}

\noindent The proof is given in Appendix~\ref{apx:technical-proofs}.

The estimate compares the best value in the ensemble against ordinary gradient flow. Note that the Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz hypothesis is imposed on the sublevel set $S_0$ itself, and Theorem~\ref{thm:ensemble-min} should be read as a no-slowdown guarantee for the ensemble's leading particle under that hypothesis, complementary to the basin-escape mechanism of Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time}, rather than as a global convergence-rate result. Without the Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz hypothesis, $m$ remains nonincreasing by the same argument (Appendix~\ref{apx:technical-proofs}); it is only the exponential rate of convergence specifically to the global value $f_\star$ that requires it.

\begin{remark}[Global existence]
    \label{rem:global-existence}
    Theorem~\ref{thm:ensemble-min} presupposes a global solution. Since $m$ is nonincreasing, every particle attaining the running minimum stays in the compact sublevel set $S_0$ when $f$ is coercive; the theorem controls these particles only. Maximizing particles ($\prefactor{k}{\xx(t)}<0$) are not controlled by this estimate, but they are damped strongly enough that blow-up is easy to exclude. Write $u_k(t):=f(x_k(t))-m(t)\ge0$. From $\sum_je^{-\beta_tf(x_j)}\ge e^{-\beta_tm}$ we get $w_k\le e^{-\beta_tu_k}$, and hence $\beta_t\bigl(J_{\beta_t}(\xx)-m\bigr)=\beta_t\sum_jw_ju_j\le\beta_t\sum_ju_je^{-\beta_tu_j}\le n/e$. Therefore
    \[
        |\prefactor{k}{\xx(t)}|\;\le\; n\bigl(1+\beta_tu_k+n/e\bigr)e^{-\beta_tu_k}\;\le\;n\bigl(1+n/e\bigr),
    \]
    using $(1+v)e^{-v}\le1$ and $e^{-v}\le1$ for $v\ge0$. The pre-factor is thus bounded uniformly and decays exponentially in the particle's energy gap, so for coercive $f$ whose gradient grows at most polynomially in $f$ the drift stays bounded along trajectories and global solutions exist. We nonetheless state the theorem for a given global solution, since its conclusion concerns only the minimizing particles.
\end{remark}


\section{Barrier Flattening and Exit Asymptotics}
\label{sec:hitting-times}

This section fixes the inverse temperature in \eqref{e:sde}, $\beta_t\equiv\beta$, with the noise term $\sqrt{2\sigma}\dd B_t$ introducing isotropic $nd$-dimensional Brownian noise. Componentwise,
\begin{equation}
    \dd x_k(t) = -\prefactor{k}{\xx_t}\nabla f(x_k(t))\dd t + \sqrt{2\sigma}\dd B_t^k,
    \label{eq:softmin-langevin}
\end{equation}
so $\prefactor{k}{\xx_t}$ acts as a state-dependent, sign-changing effective learning rate for particle $x_k$, as established in Section~\ref{sec:gradient-flow-dynamics}. The rigorous exit analysis below is carried out for physical dimension $d=1$; the extension to $d>1$ is discussed in Remark~\ref{rem:higher-dim}.


\subsection{One-dimensional basin exit and post-saddle passage}
\label{sec:exit-times}



Fix $n\ge2$, $\beta>0$, and $d=1$. Consider three consecutive nondegenerate critical points of $f$,
\[
    z_-<s<z_+,
\]
with $z_\pm$ local minima and $s$ the separating local maximum, and assume
\begin{equation}
    f'(x)>0 \text{ on } (z_-,s), \qquad f'(x)<0 \text{ on } (s,z_+).
    \label{eq:adjacent-well-monotonicity}
\end{equation}
Set the relative energy and the barrier height
\begin{equation}
    r(x):=f(x)-f(z_-), \qquad h:=r(s)>0, \qquad \rho_+:=r(z_+)<h.
    \label{eq:relative-energy}
\end{equation}
The case of interest is $\rho_+<0$, in which $z_+$ is a strictly deeper minimum than $z_-$.

\begin{assumption}
\label{assum:channel}
$f\in C^2(\R)$; $z_-<s<z_+$ are nondegenerate critical points of $f$ (i.e.\ $f''(z_-)>0$, $f''(s)<0$, $f''(z_+)>0$) satisfying the monotonicity condition \eqref{eq:adjacent-well-monotonicity}; and $n\ge2$, $\beta>0$, $d=1$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{remark}[Metastability of the initial state]
\label{rem:metastable-init}
At the consensus configuration $\bar\xx=(z_-,\dots,z_-)$, every Gibbs weight equals $1/n$, so $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}\equiv1$ for every $k$. By Corollary~\ref{cor:stability-sufficient}, using $f''(z_-)>0$, $\bar\xx$ is therefore a strict local minimum of $nJ_\beta$ and a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium of \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}, i.e.\ genuinely metastable for the noisy dynamics \eqref{e:sde} at small $\sigma$.
\end{remark}

We rely on the Freidlin--Wentzell large deviation theory for exit times of small-noise diffusions from metastable domains, summarized in Appendix~\ref{apx:fw-theory}: under the standard hypotheses of that theory, the exit time $\tau_D^\sigma$ from a metastable domain $D$ satisfies $\lim_{\sigma\downarrow0}\sigma\log \E_{\xx}\tau_D^\sigma=V_\beta(\xx,\partial D)$, where $V_\beta$ is the Freidlin--Wentzell quasipotential associated with \eqref{e:sde} at fixed $\beta$.

To build an admissible escape path, freeze $n-1$ particles at $z_-$ and let one particle move:
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma(x):=(x,z_-,\dots,z_-).
    \label{eq:channel}
\end{equation}
Factoring $e^{-\beta f(z_-)}$ out of the Gibbs weights gives $J_\beta(\Gamma(x))=f(z_-)+u(r(x))/n$, that is, the exact identity
\begin{equation}
    nJ_\beta(\Gamma(x))=nf(z_-)+u(r(x)), \qquad u(r):=\frac{n\,r\,e^{-\beta r}}{\,n-1+e^{-\beta r}\,}, \quad r\in\R,
    \label{eq:reduced-potential}
\end{equation}
where $u$ is the \emph{reduced potential}. The additive constant $nf(z_-)$ does not enter $\nabla(nJ_\beta)$, and both the action and the quasipotential depend on $nJ_\beta$ only through its increments.

\begin{lemma}[Channel invariance and reduced gradient flow]
\label{lem:channel-invariant}
The set $\{\Gamma(x):x\in\R\}$ is invariant under \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}, since $f'(z_-)=0$ forces the frozen coordinates' drift to vanish identically. On this invariant manifold, the dynamics reduces to the one-dimensional gradient flow $\dot x=-W'(x)$ of the reduced potential $W(x):=u(r(x))$ of \eqref{eq:reduced-potential}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $z_-$ is a critical point of $f$, every frozen coordinate satisfies $\dot x_j=-\prefactor{j}{\Gamma(x)}f'(z_-)=0$ by \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}, so $\{\Gamma(x):x\in\R\}$ is invariant. Differentiating \eqref{eq:reduced-potential} along the channel gives $W'(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[nJ_\beta(\Gamma(x))\bigr]=u'(r(x))r'(x)=u'(r(x))f'(x)$; applying the chain rule directly to $nJ_\beta$ and \eqref{eq:softmin-gradient} gives the same derivative as $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}f'(x)$. Hence $W'(x)=\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}f'(x)$, matching the active coordinate's drift $-\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}f'(x)$ in \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}, and in particular reproving $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}=u'(r(x))$, made explicit in closed form in Lemma~\ref{lem:channel-drift} below.
\end{proof}

The reduced potential also records the sign of the drift of the active particle, and this is what fixes the geometry of the channel.

\begin{lemma}[Channel drift and shape of the reduced potential]
\label{lem:channel-drift}
Let $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}$ denote the pre-factor of the active particle along the channel. Then, for every $x\in\R$,
\begin{equation}
    \prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}=u'(r(x))
    =\frac{n\,e^{-\beta r(x)}\,q(r(x))}{\bigl(n-1+e^{-\beta r(x)}\bigr)^{2}},
    \qquad
    q(r):=(n-1)(1-\beta r)+e^{-\beta r}.
    \label{eq:a1-equals-uprime}
\end{equation}
The function $q$ is strictly decreasing on $\R$ and has a unique zero $r_c$, which satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \frac1\beta<r_c<\frac{n}{(n-1)\beta}.
    \label{eq:rc-location}
\end{equation}
Consequently $u$ is strictly increasing on $(-\infty,r_c]$ and strictly decreasing on $[r_c,\infty)$, and $r_c$ is exactly the relative-energy level at which the active particle switches from minimizing to maximizing, i.e.\ the level at which $f(x)-J_\beta(\Gamma(x))=1/\beta$.
\end{lemma}

\noindent The proof is given in Appendix~\ref{apx:technical-proofs}.

We abbreviate the two relevant channel heights by
\begin{equation}
    u_c:=u(r_c)=\max_{r\in\R}u(r),
    \qquad
    u_h:=\max_{0\le r\le h}u(r)=u\bigl(\min\{h,r_c\}\bigr).
    \label{eq:channel-heights}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[One-dimensional basin exit and post-saddle passage]
\label{thm:transition-time}
Under Assumption~\ref{assum:channel}, the following hold.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{(Uniform barrier cap.)}
    \begin{equation}
        u_c=\max_{r\in\R}u(r)<\frac{n}{e(n-1)\beta}.
        \label{eq:barrier-cap}
    \end{equation}
    Both $u_c$ and, by \eqref{eq:rc-location}, $r_c$ are of order $\beta^{-1}$, uniformly in $h$.

    \item \textbf{(Initial basin exit: quasipotential bound.)}
    The infimum of the action among channel paths joining $\Gamma(z_-)$ to $\Gamma(s)$ equals $u_h$. Consequently
    \begin{equation}
        V_\beta\bigl(\Gamma(z_-),\Gamma(s)\bigr)\le u_h<\min\{h,r_c\}\le h.
        \label{eq:initial-bound}
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{(Post-saddle well and passage.)}
    Assume
    \begin{equation}
        r_c<h.
        \label{eq:high-barrier-regime}
    \end{equation}
    Then $\Gamma(s)$ is a strict local minimum of $nJ_\beta$, with channel barrier $u_c-u(h)$ on the $z_-$ side. On the $z_+$ side:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item If $\rho_+<r_c$, there is a unique $\xi_+\in(s,z_+)$ with $r(\xi_+)=r_c$, the channel well at $\Gamma(s)$ has matching barrier height $u_c-u(h)$ on this side, and
        \begin{equation}
            V_\beta\bigl(\Gamma(s),\Gamma(\xi_+)\bigr)\le u_c-u(h)<u_c;
            \label{eq:post-saddle-bound}
        \end{equation}
        \item If instead $\rho_+\ge r_c$, no point $\xi_+$ exists and the channel potential rises monotonically from $\Gamma(s)$ to $\Gamma(z_+)$, so no intermediate barrier separates them, with total rise $u(\rho_+)-u(h)\le u_c-u(h)$.
    \end{itemize}
    When $\rho_+<r_c$, concatenating the two crossings gives
    \begin{equation}
        V_\beta\bigl(\Gamma(z_-),\Gamma(\xi_+)\bigr) \;\le\; u_h+\bigl(u_c-u(h)\bigr) \;\le\; 2u_c-u(h) \;<\; \frac{2n}{e(n-1)\beta},
        \label{eq:two-stage-bound}
    \end{equation}
    by \eqref{eq:initial-bound}, \eqref{eq:post-saddle-bound}, and $u_h\le u_c$. Under the standing hypothesis $r_c<h$ of this part one has $u_h=u_c$, so the second inequality in \eqref{eq:two-stage-bound} is in fact an equality.
    If instead $h\le r_c$, $\Gamma(s)$ is not a local minimum of $nJ_\beta$ and no post-saddle well forms.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\noindent The proof is given in Appendix~\ref{apx:technical-proofs}.

The scope of Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} and its relation to the exact exit-time exponent is discussed in Remarks~\ref{rem:scope}--\ref{rem:higher-dim} below.

\begin{corollary}[Exit-time consequence, conditional]
\label{cor:exit-time-conditional}
Suppose in addition that $z_-$ admits a metastable domain $D_-\subset\R^n$ for the flow \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}, in the sense that $\Gamma(z_-)$ is the unique attractor of $D_-$ for the noiseless flow and the hypotheses of the Freidlin--Wentzell exit theorem (Appendix~\ref{apx:fw-theory}) hold on $D_-$, with $\Gamma(x_m)\in\partial D_-$ realizing the minimal quasipotential to $\partial D_-$, where $x_m\in(z_-,s]$ is the unique point with $r(x_m)=\min\{h,r_c\}$. Then
\[
    \lim_{\sigma\downarrow0}\sigma\log \E_{\Gamma(z_-)}\tau_{D_-}^\sigma
    =V_\beta\bigl(\Gamma(z_-),\partial D_-\bigr)\le u_h<h.
\]
\end{corollary}
We do not construct $D_-$ or verify these hypotheses here; Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} supplies only the quasipotential bound \eqref{eq:initial-bound}, which holds unconditionally.


\begin{remark}[Low-barrier regime: quasipotential reduction without sign reversal]
\label{rem:low-barrier}
By \eqref{eq:a1-equals-uprime}, $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}>0$ precisely when $r(x)<r_c$. Hence $u_h=u(h)$, i.e.\ $\min\{h,r_c\}=h$, exactly in the regime $h\le r_c$, and there the drift of the active particle is minimizing along the entire crossing: the sign-reversal mechanism is never activated, the crossing is made entirely against the drift, and by Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time}(3) no Softmin-induced well appears at $s$. The quasipotential reduction is nonetheless strict, since $u(r)/r=ne^{-\beta r}/(n-1+e^{-\beta r})<1$ for $r>0$ gives $u(h)<h$. In this regime the gain comes purely from the Gibbs-weight damping of the leading particle's contribution to $nJ_\beta$, not from a sign reversal. By \eqref{eq:rc-location} the regime is $h\lesssim\beta^{-1}$; the sign-reversal mechanism is what handles barriers beyond that scale.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Interpretation and scope]
\label{rem:scope}
Part 1 shows that, along this channel, an arbitrarily high physical barrier $h$ is flattened to a level of order $\beta^{-1}$: as $h$ grows the active particle receives exponentially small Gibbs weight, so the barrier saturates rather than growing with $h$. Part 2 gives a strict full-system \emph{upper} bound on the exit exponent through an explicit admissible path; because $V_\beta$ is an infimum over all paths in $\R^n$, the channel construction can only over-estimate it. It is a bound on the Freidlin--Wentzell quasipotential itself, established by exhibiting an admissible path; it does not yet assert anything about $\E\tau^\sigma$, for which see Corollary~\ref{cor:exit-time-conditional}. Part 3 addresses the post-saddle obstruction: a high barrier turns the physical maximum $s$ into a Softmin-induced well. When $\rho_+<r_c$ this well has equal-height barriers, with depth $u_c-u(h)$ strictly below the initial barrier $u_c$, so it is not a worse obstruction than the one already crossed, giving the composite two-stage bound \eqref{eq:two-stage-bound}; when $\rho_+\ge r_c$ the $z_+$ side presents no intermediate barrier at all, and its monotone rise $u(\rho_+)-u(h)$ is itself bounded by $u_c-u(h)$, so this case is only more favorable. We emphasize that \eqref{eq:two-stage-bound} bounds a quasipotential, for which the two costs do add. If $\Gamma(s)$ were itself metastable in the sense of Corollary~\ref{cor:exit-time-conditional}, so that the two crossings are traversed as successive escapes each restarting the clock, the resulting exit-time exponent would instead be governed by the larger of them, $\max\{u_h,u_c-u(h)\}\le u_c$, since the dominant crossing would set the asymptotic rate; we do not establish that here. The theorem does \emph{not} identify the exact transition exponent between the two basins, which would require ruling out returns to the initial basin and competing collective paths in $\R^n$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Comparison with independent Langevin dynamics]
\label{rem:comparison}
For a single overdamped Langevin particle in the potential $f$ started at $z_-$, the classical small-noise exit exponent from the basin of $z_-$ is the physical barrier $h=f(s)-f(z_-)$. For $n$ \emph{independent} copies, the first of the $n$ exit times has the same exponential scale $h$: taking a minimum over $n$ i.i.d.\ variables with common exponential rate $h/\sigma$ changes only the multiplicative pre-factor, by a factor $1/n$, not the exponent, as $\sigma\downarrow0$.

By \eqref{eq:initial-bound}, the channel construction gives $V_\beta(\Gamma(z_-),\Gamma(s))\le u_h<h$ for the joint Softmin diffusion, in \emph{every} regime of $h$ (see Remark~\ref{rem:low-barrier}). This is a comparison of an \emph{upper bound on a quasipotential} for the interacting system against the \emph{exact} exponent $h$ of the noninteracting one; it shows the admissible channel gives a strictly lower quasipotential bound than independent escape, not that the true joint exit exponent is smaller. The stronger, exit-\emph{time} statement that the interaction cannot slow escape holds only under the hypotheses of Corollary~\ref{cor:exit-time-conditional}, which are not verified in this paper.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Higher dimensions, temperature, and scale]
\label{rem:higher-dim}
The channel identity of Part 1 extends algebraically to $d>1$: at a critical point $s$ of $f$ the active Hessian block is $u'(h)\nabla^2 f(s)$, so if $s$ is an index-one saddle and $u'(h)<0$ the induced local minimum is a genuinely one-dimensional phenomenon, and transverse unstable directions may open cheaper escape routes. Although Theorem~\ref{thm:ensemble-min} holds for an arbitrary schedule $\beta_t$, and Section~\ref{sec:hitting-times} fixed $\beta$ purely for tractability of the exit-time analysis, a natural question is whether annealing $\beta$ helps in practice. Regarding temperature, the fixed-$\beta$ bound \eqref{eq:barrier-cap} decreases like $\beta^{-1}$, favoring large $\beta$ for barrier reduction; but large $\beta$ also suppresses the drift of non-leading particles through their Gibbs weights, since a particle trailing the ensemble minimum by a gap $\Delta$ is weighted by $e^{-\beta\Delta}$. Both limits degrade the mechanism: as $\beta\to\infty$ the ensemble degenerates to leader-only drift, while as $\beta\to0$ the Softmin coupling degenerates toward independent noisy gradient descent and \eqref{eq:barrier-cap}'s bound itself diverges. A decaying (or, symmetrically, an increasing) schedule $\beta_t$ is a natural candidate for balancing these two effects over time; however, we found empirically that neither improves on a well-chosen \emph{fixed} $\beta$ on the analytic benchmarks considered here. Both a geometric decay from a large starting $\beta$ and a geometric growth from a small one consistently reach the target \emph{more slowly} than a fixed $\beta$ tuned once via the diagnostic of Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparameters}, with the gap widening at harder barrier heights, and an aggressive growth rate can drive $\beta$ to numerical overflow before the schedule ever reaches a useful range. We report this as a negative empirical result rather than infer a general claim about annealing from it: a rigorous large-deviation treatment of $\beta_t$, and of whether some other schedule shape or landscape can realize the exploration/refinement trade-off this remark motivates, remain open.
\end{remark}

\section{Numerical Experiments}
\label{sec:numerical-experiments}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:softmin} states the discretized method exactly as run.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Softmin energy dynamics: Euler--Maruyama discretization of \eqref{e:sde}.}
\label{alg:softmin}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require objective $f$ and gradient $\nabla f$; ensemble size $n$; inverse temperature $\beta$; step size $\eta$; noise intensity $\sigma$; box $B$ with coordinatewise projection $\Pi_B$; horizon $T$
\State draw $x_1,\dots,x_n$ i.i.d.\ uniformly on the initialization region (Table~\ref{tab:general_settings})
\For{$t=1,\dots,T$}
    \State $f_k\gets f(x_k)$ for every $k$, and $f_\ast\gets\min_j f_j$
    \State $w_k\gets e^{-\beta(f_k-f_\ast)}\bigl/\sum_{j}e^{-\beta(f_j-f_\ast)}$ \Comment{shifted for numerical stability}
    \State $J\gets\sum_k w_kf_k$ \Comment{Softmin energy \eqref{eq:Softmin-energy}}
    \State $\prefactor{k}{\xx}\gets nw_k\bigl[1-\beta(f_k-J)\bigr]$ for every $k$ \Comment{pre-factor \eqref{eq:softmin-gradient}}
    \State $x_k\gets\Pi_B\bigl(x_k-\eta\,\prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla f(x_k)+\sqrt{2\sigma\eta}\,\xi_k\bigr)$, \ $\xi_k\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_d)$ i.i.d.
\EndFor
\State \Return $\argmin_{x_k}f(x_k)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

We compare Softmin, at a fixed inverse temperature, against seven baselines: Simulated Annealing
\cite{gelfand1990sampling}; overdamped Langevin dynamics \cite{welling2011bayesian};
Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO) using gradient information, with and without annealing
\cite{carrillo2021consensus}; Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) using gradient information
\cite{noel2012new}; and the canonical gradient-free forms of CBO
\cite{CarrilloChoiTotzeckTse2018, pinnau2017consensus} and PSO \cite{kennedy1995particle}. Each
benchmark makes one thing hard at a time: the height of the barrier between a shallow and a deep
basin (Section~\ref{sec:double-quadruple-well}), the number of local minima as the dimension grows
(Sections~\ref{sec:lj-benchmark} and~\ref{sec:ackley-benchmark}), the size of the ensemble
(Section~\ref{sec:particle-sweep}), and the training of a neural network
(Section~\ref{sec:transformer}). Throughout, we report two quantities together: \emph{reliability},
the fraction of independent runs that reach the target, and \emph{speed}, the time a successful run
takes. Speed alone would mislead, since a method that often fails can look fast on the few runs it
solves. Reporting conventions and detailed results are given in
Appendix~\ref{apx:experiment-details}, settings and hyperparameter calibration in
Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparameters}, and the source code is available at
\cite{softmin_energy_2026}.

One caveat applies throughout. Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} describes the limit of vanishing
noise, whereas the analytic benchmarks run at a noise level at which the barrier cap it proves is
negligible compared with the noise, and the transformer benchmark, which uses a far smaller noise, is
not the one-dimensional setting the theorem describes. The experiments therefore test whether Softmin helps in a practically
relevant regime, and where they agree with the theory we claim consistency, not confirmation. The
regime the theorem does describe is tested separately in Appendix~\ref{apx:theory-verification}.

\subsection{Double-well and quadruple-well benchmarks}
\label{sec:double-quadruple-well}

The one-dimensional double well and the two-dimensional quadruple well each have a shallow basin,
where every run starts, and a deeper one, the target, separated by a barrier whose height we
control. Raising the barrier makes escape rapidly harder for methods driven by noise, which is the
situation Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} addresses.

\begin{figure*}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/well_benchmarks.png}

  \vspace{0.5\baselineskip}
  \caption{Double well (left, against barrier height) and quadruple well (right, against the depth
  gap between the two wells). Top: fraction of the 100 runs reaching the target, at every point of
  the sweep. Bottom: expected cost of a success, in function and gradient evaluations, counting the
  cost of failed runs; a gap means no run succeeded. The time to reach the target over successful
  runs alone is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:well-entry-time}, and conventions are detailed in
  Appendix~\ref{apx:protocol}.}
  \label{fig:experiments}
  \vspace{\baselineskip}
\end{figure*}

Softmin reaches the target in every run at every barrier height on both landscapes
(Figure~\ref{fig:experiments}, top), across barriers spanning three orders of magnitude. Every
baseline eventually fails. Simulated Annealing matches Softmin while the barrier is low and then
collapses until no run succeeds; Langevin dynamics fails much earlier; CBO and PSO, with or without
gradients, almost never reach the target at any difficulty. This is the pattern
Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} describes: a method whose effective barrier is capped
independently of the true height keeps escaping, while methods governed by the true height fail
once it is large enough. On the double well, Softmin's cost of reaching the target also grows far
more slowly than the barrier itself (Figure~\ref{fig:experiments}, bottom left). For the reason given above we read this as consistency with the theory, not as
evidence that its mechanism drives the result; an ablation (Appendix~\ref{apx:ablation-study})
points to the Gibbs weighting of the ensemble as the more plausible source.

Where Simulated Annealing still succeeds, it is faster than Softmin, and we do not claim otherwise
(Figure~\ref{fig:well-entry-time}).
Speed on solved runs is not the price of a solution, however, because a practitioner cannot know in
advance which runs will fail. Charging each method for its failed runs as well, as is standard in
black-box optimization benchmarking \cite{hansen2016coco}, turns the comparison into a crossover
(Figure~\ref{fig:experiments}, bottom).
Simulated Annealing is the cheaper method on low barriers, but beyond a moderate barrier its
expected cost grows without bound: its cooling schedule freezes the dynamics after a fixed time,
whatever the barrier, and a longer budget does not rescue it. Softmin's cost also rises with
difficulty, but by several orders of magnitude less than Simulated Annealing's, and over the whole
sweep Softmin is more than an order of magnitude cheaper than
Simulated Annealing (Appendix~\ref{apx:wells}).

\subsection{Lennard-Jones cluster benchmark}
\label{sec:lj-benchmark}

Lennard-Jones clusters are a standard, physically motivated test of global optimization in higher
dimension: $N$ atoms interact through a pairwise potential \cite{jones1924determination}, and the
number of local minima grows exponentially with $N$. We use clusters of 13 to 75 atoms, up to 225
dimensions, whose best known energies are catalogued in the Cambridge Cluster Database
\cite{wales1997global}. As is standard \cite{wales1997global}, each method's final configuration is
relaxed to its nearest local minimum before scoring, so the comparison measures which basin a method
found rather than how precisely it converged within it. We score the energy gap to the best known
minimum rather than a hitting time, since beyond the smallest cluster almost no method, Softmin
included, reaches a strict target within the budget.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{images/lj_energy_gap.png}
  \vspace{\baselineskip}
  \caption{Lennard-Jones clusters of 13 to 75 atoms: energy gap between the best configuration each
  method finds, after relaxation to its nearest local minimum, and the best known minimum.}
  \label{fig:lj-experiments}
  \vspace{\baselineskip}
\end{figure}

Softmin's gap stays small and nearly constant as the cluster grows, while every baseline's gap grows
roughly in proportion to the number of atoms (Figure~\ref{fig:lj-experiments}). Softmin's advantage
therefore widens with dimension rather than narrowing, contrary to the usual intuition that
high-dimensional search erodes an optimizer's edge. Details are in Appendix~\ref{apx:lj}.

\subsection{High-dimensional Ackley benchmark}
\label{sec:ackley-benchmark}

The generalized Ackley function places a dense lattice of shallow local minima on a single smooth
funnel; we test it in 10 to 90 dimensions. It is the benchmark on which Softmin performs worst, and
we report it in full.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{images/ackley_dimensions.png}
  \vspace{\baselineskip}
  \caption{Generalized Ackley function in 10 to 90 dimensions: time to reach the target, averaged
  over successful runs, with the conventions of Appendix~\ref{apx:protocol}. Softmin is missing in
  10 and 20 dimensions, where it never reaches the target; Langevin dynamics and canonical CBO are
  missing everywhere for the same reason.}
  \label{fig:ackley-experiments}
\end{figure}

Particle Swarm Optimization is the fastest method at every dimension, by a wide margin
(Figure~\ref{fig:ackley-experiments}). Its gradient-free form does just as well as the
gradient-enhanced one, so what separates the methods here is their search dynamics rather than
access to the gradient, which makes Ackley a weak test of the question this paper is about. Outside
the PSO family, Softmin is the only method that becomes reliable as the dimension grows: it fails in
the two lowest dimensions, but from 40 dimensions on it reaches the target in every run, and in the
highest dimension it is the most reliable method tested.

The trend with dimension also favors Softmin. Because Ackley averages over coordinates, its barriers
shrink as the dimension grows; Softmin's time to the target falls with them, and its gap to PSO
closes by several orders of magnitude across the sweep. This is consistent with the barrier
flattening of Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time}, and again we claim no more than consistency. The
headline nonetheless stands: PSO is faster at every dimension. Ackley marks the boundary of our
claim, since on a landscape whose difficulty lies in the number of minima rather than their depth,
Softmin delivers reliability but not speed. Why PSO does so well here we leave open. Details are in
Appendix~\ref{apx:ackley}.

\subsection{Scaling with ensemble size}
\label{sec:particle-sweep}

Every benchmark above uses 100 particles. Here we vary the ensemble size on the quadruple well, from
5 to 400 particles, comparing Softmin with Simulated Annealing at three depth gaps between the wells. The
comparison is sharper than it looks: Simulated Annealing with $n$ particles is $n$ independent runs,
so a larger ensemble gives it more restarts, and its reliability may improve for that reason alone.
The question is therefore whether Softmin's coupling of the particles achieves something that
independent restarts do not.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/particle_sweep.png}
  \vspace{\baselineskip}
  \caption{Softmin and Simulated Annealing on the quadruple well at an intermediate depth gap, with
  5 to 400 particles. Left: fraction of runs reaching the target; the dashed line is what
  independent restarts predict from the smallest ensemble alone. Center: time to the target,
  averaged over successful runs only. Right: expected cost of a success, counting failed runs.
  The other difficulties and further details are in Appendix~\ref{apx:particle-sweep}.}
  \label{fig:particle-sweep}
\end{figure}

Softmin reaches the target in every run at every ensemble size and difficulty, even with 5
particles. Simulated Annealing splits by difficulty. At the smallest depth gap it also always succeeds.
At the intermediate one, its reliability grows with the ensemble exactly as independent restarts
predict (Figure~\ref{fig:particle-sweep}, left): a single per-run success probability, estimated
from the smallest ensemble, accounts for every larger one. Its gain from the ensemble is restarts and
nothing more, and matching Softmin with five independent runs would require each run to succeed far
more often than Simulated Annealing's do. At the largest depth gap, no ensemble size rescues it.
Langevin dynamics makes the role of the coupling sharper still. With a single particle, Softmin's
pre-factor is identically one and its update is exactly that of Langevin dynamics with the same step
size and noise, which fails at all three depth gaps even with 100 independent particles
(Figure~\ref{fig:experiments}). Five Softmin particles therefore differ from five independent copies
of a method that fails only in their interaction, so the difference in outcome is attributable to
that interaction. This contrast is again what Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} suggests: restarts multiply a success
probability without changing how fast it decays with the barrier, so they help at a moderate barrier
but not at a large one, whereas a method whose effective barrier is capped should need them at
neither. As before, we claim consistency only.

The two methods use the ensemble for opposite purposes. Counting failed runs, Softmin is cheapest
with its smallest ensemble at every difficulty, since it needs no extra particles to be reliable and
pays for each one. Simulated Annealing's expected cost instead falls as the ensemble grows, wherever
the extra restarts buy reliability it otherwise lacks. The outcome depends on difficulty, as on the
wells. At the smallest depth gap Simulated Annealing is cheaper at every ensemble size. At the
intermediate one Softmin is far cheaper with small ensembles, but from 100 particles on Simulated
Annealing succeeds in every run and the two cost about the same, within a factor of two either way,
so this advantage can be overcome by brute force, which we record as a limitation
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). At the largest depth gap Simulated Annealing's cost is effectively
unbounded. Finally, Softmin's time to the target at the largest depth gap varies with ensemble size in a way we cannot explain; the effect reproduces on a
fresh set of runs and does not affect reliability, and we analyse it in
Appendix~\ref{apx:particle-sweep}.

\subsection{Training a transformer}
\label{sec:transformer}

The benchmarks above are analytic landscapes whose barriers are known exactly. As a test on a real,
high-dimensional problem, we use Softmin to train the weights of a small transformer on modular
addition, a standard testbed for small transformers \cite{power2022grokking,nanda2023progress}
that is also used to compare optimizers \cite{tveit2025muon}. We vary the depth of the model from 2
to 50 layers, so the number of parameters being optimized grows from about $10^5$ to
$2.5\times10^6$. Besides Simulated Annealing and Langevin dynamics, we compare against Adam
\cite{kingma2014adam} and SGD with momentum, the standard optimizers for this problem. Every method
uses 10 particles and must bring the training loss below $0.10$. The task, model, calibration and
a more detailed analysis are given in Appendix~\ref{apx:transformer}.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{images/transformer_depth.png}
  \vspace{\baselineskip}
  \caption{Transformer training at depths from 2 to 50 layers, five seeds per method and depth.
  Steps needed to reach the target training loss, as the mean over seeds (line) and for each seed
  (faint points); markers are offset horizontally so that they do not overlap. Details in
  Appendix~\ref{apx:transformer}.}
  \label{fig:transformer}
\end{figure}

Every method reaches the target from every starting point at every depth, so this benchmark does not separate the methods on
reliability. Nor did greater depth make training harder: every baseline reaches the target sooner
in the deepest model than in the shallowest. What the benchmark does measure is speed at scale
(Figure~\ref{fig:transformer}). Softmin trains every model it is given, and the number of
steps it needs barely changes with depth. It needs about a quarter of the steps of Simulated
Annealing and fewer than Langevin dynamics at every depth. SGD, which speeds up as the model gets
deeper, starts out twice as slow as Softmin and matches it at 50 layers, while Adam is three to
four and a half times faster than Softmin throughout. Measured in wall-clock time, where a Softmin
step is somewhat more expensive, SGD is already ahead from 5 layers on.

We therefore read this benchmark as evidence that Softmin carries over to neural-network training
with millions of parameters, not as evidence of an advantage there. As on Ackley
(Section~\ref{sec:ackley-benchmark}), Softmin is not the fastest method. This is consistent with
where its advantage should lie: when every method reaches the target from every start, a mechanism
for escaping poor basins has nothing to act on. Whether Softmin separates from Adam on a training
problem where runs do fail remains open.

\section{Conclusion}

In this work, we introduced a method for escaping local minima based on the Softmin energy interaction dynamics, and established two guarantees for it. Without noise, the running best objective value across the ensemble is nonincreasing and, whenever $f$ satisfies a Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition on the initial sublevel set, converges to the global minimum at least as fast as ordinary gradient flow, for any inverse-temperature schedule (Theorem~\ref{thm:ensemble-min}): the Softmin coupling never slows down the leading particle. At fixed temperature, the interaction caps the Freidlin--Wentzell quasipotential barrier between adjacent basins at a level of order $1/\beta$, independently of the true barrier height (Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time}).

The experiments relate to these results unevenly, and it is worth being precise about how. None of them tests the ensemble-descent guarantee, which concerns the noiseless flow. The barrier cap is examined in the small-noise regime it is stated for (Appendix~\ref{apx:theory-verification}), where the measured exit times are consistent with it. At the noise level used for the analytic benchmarks, however, the cap is too small to be the operative effect, and an ablation (Appendix~\ref{apx:ablation-study}) finds that at $\beta=10$ the sign-reversal mechanism is not what carries the empirical advantage, though it appears to become dominant at $\beta=2$ as the barrier grows. The benchmarks therefore show the qualitative behavior the theory describes, not evidence that its mechanism produces it.

Within those bounds, the empirical case for Softmin is its reliability. On the double-well and quadruple-well benchmarks it is the only method that keeps reaching the target as the barrier grows, in every run across both sweeps, while Simulated Annealing and Langevin dynamics collapse at a finite difficulty and the Consensus-Based and Particle Swarm variants almost never succeed. Because it never exhausts its budget, it is also more than an order of magnitude cheaper per solution than Simulated Annealing over the sweep, although Simulated Annealing is cheaper on low barriers. This reliability is not an effect of ensemble size: Softmin keeps it with as few as five particles, whereas Simulated Annealing gains from more particles only what independent restarts predict, which recovers its reliability on moderate barriers but not on high ones. The method also carries over to higher dimension. On Lennard-Jones clusters, Softmin's quenched-energy gap to the best known minimum stays small and nearly flat as the cluster grows, while every baseline's grows roughly linearly, and on transformer training it runs with the same $\beta$ at up to $2.5\times10^6$ parameters, needing fewer steps than Simulated Annealing and Langevin dynamics at every depth.

Two benchmarks mark where this advantage ends. On the high-dimensional Ackley function, Particle Swarm Optimization is faster than Softmin at every dimension tested, and Softmin does not reach the target at all below $d=30$. Softmin does become reliable as the dimension grows and the landscape's barriers shrink, reaching the target in every run from $d=40$ on and being the most reliable method at the highest dimension; but barrier flattening is not sufficient for competitive speed when difficulty is carried by the density of local minima rather than by their depth. On transformer training, every method reached the target in every run, so that benchmark does not probe reliability, and there Adam is several times faster than Softmin.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Works}
\label{sec:limitations}
Our theoretical analysis provides rigorous guarantees in the small-noise regime where the Freidlin--Wentzell Large Deviation Principles apply. While the bound in Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} is not sharp -- in particular $u_c$ and $u_h$ are only upper-estimated by an explicit channel, not computed exactly for general $f$ -- a tighter characterization of the true quasipotential, together with intermediate-noise asymptotics, would sharpen the picture. Our equilibrium characterization (Theorem~\ref{thm:stationary-points}) requires no convexity assumption on $f$ whatsoever; the ensemble-descent estimate (Theorem~\ref{thm:ensemble-min}) guarantees the running minimum is nonincreasing regardless, but its exponential rate of convergence specifically to the global minimum requires a Polyak–\L{}ojasiewicz condition on the relevant sublevel set, as discussed after its statement. The exit-time consequence of Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} holds unconditionally only at the level of the quasipotential, and becomes an actual statement about $\E\tau^\sigma$ only under the unverified metastable-domain hypotheses of Corollary~\ref{cor:exit-time-conditional}; identifying the exact transition exponent between adjacent basins, and a rigorous large-deviation treatment of the annealed schedule $\beta_t$, remain open.

A second gap is empirical, between what the theory describes and what the benchmarks measure. The analytic benchmarks run at $\sigma=1$, where the cap of \eqref{eq:barrier-cap} evaluates to $u_c\approx0.037$ and so is negligible against the noise; the ablation of Appendix~\ref{apx:ablation-study} correspondingly finds that at $\beta=10$ the sign-reversal mechanism the theory is built on is not what produces the observed advantage, with the Gibbs weighting shared by all three ablation variants the more plausible source. The mechanism does appear to become dominant at the lower temperature $\beta=2$ as barriers grow, consistently with the theory; but establishing that the proven mechanism drives the headline results would require either running the benchmarks down toward the small-noise regime or a control that removes the drift entirely, neither of which we do here. The Ackley results of Section~\ref{sec:ackley-benchmark} bound the claim from the other side. There the sign-reversal mechanism is active -- the landscape's barriers sit well above $r_c$ -- and Softmin's performance improves with dimension as those barriers shrink, yet PSO is faster at every dimension, on a benchmark where gradient information demonstrably contributes nothing. Barrier flattening is therefore not sufficient for competitive speed on a landscape whose difficulty is carried by the density of minima rather than by their depth. Why PSO in particular does so well here we leave open: we did not isolate the responsible mechanism, and the comparison identifies the limitation without explaining it. The ensemble-size sweep of Section~\ref{sec:particle-sweep} bounds the reliability claim from a third side. At the moderate depth gap $A=20$, Simulated Annealing's reliability is recovered in full by restarts alone once $n\geq100$, and from there its expected cost to solution sits within about a factor of two of Softmin's; the advantage we report at the paper's default $n=100$ is therefore specific to difficulties at which restarts have stopped working, and is not a uniform property of the method at every barrier height. The transformer benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:transformer} bounds it from a fourth side: every method reached the target on every seed at every depth, so our experiments contain no test of reliability on a neural-network objective, and on that benchmark Adam is several times faster than Softmin, while SGD catches up with it in steps as depth grows and is faster in wall-clock time from depth 5 on.

Future work should address several important directions. First, extending convergence proofs and explicit convergence rates beyond the Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz setting, particularly to objectives with multiple disconnected basins, would significantly broaden applicability. Second, analyzing the mean-field limit as $n \to \infty$ could provide insights into the scaling of the method with ensemble size and potentially yield dimension-free convergence rates; Section~\ref{sec:particle-sweep} measures that scaling empirically over $5\leq n\leq400$ and finds reliability already saturated at the bottom of that range, which such an analysis would need to explain. Third, although Theorem~\ref{thm:ensemble-min} guarantees the ensemble-descent bound for an arbitrary schedule $\beta_t$, our experiments (Remark~\ref{rem:higher-dim}) found that neither a decaying nor an increasing schedule improved on a well-chosen fixed $\beta$ on the benchmarks considered, contrary to the original motivation for annealing $\beta$ over time; understanding whether this is a property of these specific landscapes or a more general phenomenon, and whether an adaptive schedule driven by the ensemble's own state (rather than a fixed function of $t$) can do better, is an open, concrete direction. Fourth, our neural-network benchmark turned out to be one on which every method succeeds; whether Softmin's reliability advantage carries over to network-training problems on which runs do fail, where it could separate from Adam, remains untested. Finally, tighter estimates for the exponential rate in the small noise regime can sharpen the theoretical bound to match experimental evidence more closely.

\section{Freidlin--Wentzell Large Deviation Theory}
\label{apx:fw-theory}

This appendix summarizes the background large deviation theory used by Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} and Corollary~\ref{cor:exit-time-conditional}. Consider a small-noise gradient diffusion on $\R^N$,
\[
    \dd\XX_t = -\nabla U(\XX_t)\dd t + \sqrt{2\sigma}\dd B_t,
\]
for a potential $U\in C^2(\R^N)$ and standard $N$-dimensional Brownian motion $(B_t)_{t\ge0}$; in our application $N=nd$ and $U=nJ_\beta$. As $\sigma\downarrow0$, sample paths of this diffusion concentrate around trajectories of the noiseless flow $\dot\XX_t=-\nabla U(\XX_t)$, and rare deviations from this flow are governed by the Freidlin--Wentzell large deviation principle \cite{freidlin2012random}. For an absolutely continuous path $\phi:[0,T]\to\R^N$, the Freidlin--Wentzell action is
\begin{equation}
    I_{0T}(\phi):=\frac14\int_0^T\bigl\|\dot\phi(t)+\nabla U(\phi(t))\bigr\|^2\dd t,
    \label{eq:fw-action}
\end{equation}
and the quasipotential between a point $x\in\R^N$ and a closed set $A\subset\R^N$ is
\begin{equation}
    V(x,A):=\inf_{T>0}\ \inf_{\substack{\phi(0)=x\\ \phi(T)\in A}}I_{0T}(\phi).
    \label{eq:fw-quasipotential}
\end{equation}
Intuitively, $I_{0T}(\phi)$ penalizes deviation from the noiseless flow, vanishing exactly along trajectories of $\dot\phi=-\nabla U(\phi)$; $V(x,A)$ is the cheapest such penalty over all paths from $x$ reaching $A$, and quantifies how atypical (large-deviation-improbable) it is for the diffusion to travel from $x$ to $A$ without the help of noise. The infimum in \eqref{eq:fw-quasipotential} need not be attained by any single finite-time path: it is generally only approximated arbitrarily well, e.g.\ by following the time-reversed noiseless flow toward $x$ and then the forward flow toward $A$, taking arbitrarily long to approach the relevant critical points. This is the sense in which the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} below computes exactly the minimal action along one specific channel of paths; the quasipotential itself is not computed, but bounded from above by that value, since it is an infimum over all paths.

Under the standard hypotheses of Freidlin--Wentzell exit theory \cite{freidlin2012random} -- in particular that $D$ is a bounded domain with $x\in D$, that $x$ is the unique asymptotically stable equilibrium of the noiseless flow in $D$, and that $\partial D$ contains no other equilibria -- the exit time $\tau_D^\sigma$ of the diffusion from $D$, started at $x$, satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \lim_{\sigma\downarrow0}\sigma\log \E_x\tau_D^\sigma = V(x,\partial D).
    \label{eq:fw-exit-theorem}
\end{equation}

For a gradient drift, the action of any path admits the exact decomposition
\begin{equation}
    I_{0T}(\phi) = U(\phi(T))-U(\phi(0)) + \frac14\int_0^T\bigl\|\dot\phi(t)-\nabla U(\phi(t))\bigr\|^2\dd t,
    \label{eq:gradient-decomposition}
\end{equation}
obtained by expanding the square in \eqref{eq:fw-action} and completing it against $\dd U(\phi(t))/\dd t = \langle\nabla U(\phi(t)),\dot\phi(t)\rangle$. Consequently, any segment of $\phi$ that follows the noiseless flow \emph{downhill} (i.e.\ $\dot\phi=-\nabla U(\phi)$) contributes zero action, while a segment moving \emph{uphill} against the flow (i.e.\ $\dot\phi=\nabla U(\phi)$) costs exactly the potential increase along that segment. This identity is what allows the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} to compute exactly the minimal action along a specific channel of admissible paths, and hence to bound the quasipotential from above.

\section{Technical Proofs}
\label{apx:technical-proofs}

Here we will collect some of the more technical proofs of the lemmas stated in the main body.

\subsection{Proofs of Section~\ref{sec:gradient-flow-dynamics}}

\begin{lemma}[Component gradient and Hessian at critical configurations]
    \label{lem:derivative-of-J-proof}
    Let $w_k = e^{-\beta f(x_k)}(\sum_j e^{-\beta f(x_j)})^{-1}$ denote the Gibbs weight of particle $x_k$, and let $\prefactor{k}{\xx} = nw_k\left[1 - \beta(f(x_k) - J_\beta(\xx))\right]$ be the dynamic pre-factor. Then
    \begin{equation}
        \nabla_{x_k} (nJ_\beta(\xx)) = \prefactor{k}{\xx} \nabla f(x_k),
        \qquad
        \sum_{k=1}^n \prefactor{k}{\xx} = n.
        \label{eq:lemma-gradient}
    \end{equation}
    Moreover, for $k \neq \ell$ the mixed Hessian block is the outer product
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\partial^2 (nJ_\beta(\xx))}{\partial x_\ell \partial x_k} = \nabla f(x_k)\bigl(\nabla_{x_\ell}\prefactor{k}{\xx}\bigr)^\top,
        \label{eq:mixed-block}
    \end{equation}
    which vanishes whenever $\nabla f(x_k)=0$. Consequently, if $\xx$ is such that $\nabla f(x_k)=0$ for every $k$, then $\nabla(nJ_\beta(\xx))=0$ and the Hessian is block diagonal,
    \begin{equation}
        \nabla^2(nJ_\beta(\xx)) = \operatorname{blockdiag}\bigl(\prefactor{1}{\xx}\nabla^2 f(x_1),\dots,\prefactor{n}{\xx}\nabla^2 f(x_n)\bigr).
        \label{eq:block-diagonal-hessian}
    \end{equation}
    Finally, if $\prefactor{k}{\xx}=0$ then $\frac{\partial^2(nJ_\beta(\xx))}{\partial x_k^2} = -n\beta w_k \nabla f(x_k)\nabla f(x_k)^\top$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    We compute the derivatives using the chain rule and the properties of the Gibbs weights. First, we establish the gradient of the weight $w_i$ with respect to $x_k$:
    \begin{equation*}
        \nabla_{x_k} w_i = \begin{cases}
            \beta w_i w_k \nabla f(x_k) & \text{if } i \neq k, \\
            -\beta w_k (1 - w_k) \nabla f(x_k) & \text{if } i = k.
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    For the first derivative of $nJ_\beta(\xx) = n\sum_i w_i f(x_i)$, an identical computation to the main text gives $\nabla_{x_k}(nJ_\beta(\xx)) = nw_k[1-\beta(f(x_k)-J_\beta(\xx))]\nabla f(x_k) = \prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla f(x_k)$; summing over $k$ and using $\sum_k w_k=1$, $\sum_k w_kf(x_k)=J_\beta(\xx)$, gives $\sum_k\prefactor{k}{\xx}=n[1-\beta(J_\beta(\xx)-J_\beta(\xx))]=n$.

    For the mixed second derivative ($k\ne\ell$), we differentiate $\nabla_{x_k}(nJ_\beta(\xx))=\prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla f(x_k)$ with respect to $x_\ell$. Because $\nabla f(x_k)$ is independent of $x_\ell$, the Jacobian matrix is the outer product $\nabla f(x_k)(\nabla_{x_\ell}\prefactor{k}{\xx})^\top$. Substituting $\nabla_{x_\ell}w_k=\beta w_kw_\ell\nabla f(x_\ell)$ and $\nabla_{x_\ell}J_\beta(\xx)=\frac1n\prefactor{\ell}{\xx}\nabla f(x_\ell)$,
    \begin{align*}
        \nabla_{x_\ell}\prefactor{k}{\xx} &= n(\nabla_{x_\ell}w_k)\left[1-\beta(f(x_k)-J_\beta(\xx))\right] + nw_k\beta\left(\nabla_{x_\ell}J_\beta(\xx)\right) \\
        &= \beta w_\ell\bigl(nw_k[1-\beta(f(x_k)-J_\beta(\xx))]\bigr)\nabla f(x_\ell) + \beta w_k\prefactor{\ell}{\xx}\nabla f(x_\ell) \\
        &= \beta\left[w_\ell\prefactor{k}{\xx}+w_k\prefactor{\ell}{\xx}\right]\nabla f(x_\ell),
    \end{align*}
    which gives \eqref{eq:mixed-block} and vanishes whenever $\nabla f(x_k)=0$ (or symmetrically $\nabla f(x_\ell)=0$). If $\nabla f(x_k)=0$ for every $k$, every off-diagonal block vanishes and \eqref{eq:lemma-gradient} gives $\nabla(nJ_\beta(\xx))=0$; the diagonal block computed below then reduces to $\prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla^2f(x_k)$, giving the block-diagonal form \eqref{eq:block-diagonal-hessian}.

    Finally, for the diagonal block ($k=\ell$), applying the product rule to $\nabla_{x_k}(nJ_\beta(\xx))=\prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla f(x_k)$ includes the local Hessian $\nabla^2 f(x_k)$:
    \[
        \frac{\partial^2(nJ_\beta(\xx))}{\partial x_k^2} = \prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla^2f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)\bigl(\nabla_{x_k}\prefactor{k}{\xx}\bigr)^\top.
    \]
    Using $\nabla_{x_k}w_k=-\beta w_k(1-w_k)\nabla f(x_k)$ and $\nabla_{x_k}J_\beta(\xx)=\frac1n\prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla f(x_k)$, a computation identical in structure to the mixed case gives
    \[
        \nabla_{x_k}\prefactor{k}{\xx} = -\beta\left[nw_k+\prefactor{k}{\xx}-2w_k\prefactor{k}{\xx}\right]\nabla f(x_k),
    \]
    so that, substituting back,
    \[
        \frac{\partial^2(nJ_\beta(\xx))}{\partial x_k^2} = \prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla^2f(x_k) - \beta\left[nw_k+\prefactor{k}{\xx}-2w_k\prefactor{k}{\xx}\right]\nabla f(x_k)\nabla f(x_k)^\top,
    \]
    which reduces to $-n\beta w_k\nabla f(x_k)\nabla f(x_k)^\top$ when $\prefactor{k}{\xx}=0$. \qedhere
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:stationary-points}]
    A locally asymptotically stable equilibrium of the gradient flow \eqref{eq:gradient-flow} is an isolated local minimum of $nJ_\beta$: indeed $\frac{d}{dt}(nJ_\beta(\xx(t)))=-\|\nabla(nJ_\beta)\|^2\le0$, so if points arbitrarily close to $\bar\xx$ had strictly smaller value the trajectories starting there could not converge to $\bar\xx$; and a non-isolated equilibrium admits nearby constant trajectories that likewise do not converge to $\bar\xx$.

    Fix $k$ and freeze the other particles at $\bar x_j$, $j\ne k$. Since $nJ_\beta$ depends on $x_k$ only through $r=f(x_k)$, we may write
    \[
        nJ_\beta(\bar x_1,\dots,x_k,\dots,\bar x_n)=g(f(x_k)), \qquad g(r):=n\,\frac{re^{-\beta r}+C_1}{e^{-\beta r}+C_0},
    \]
    with $C_0:=\sum_{j\ne k}e^{-\beta f(\bar x_j)}>0$ and $C_1:=\sum_{j\ne k}f(\bar x_j)e^{-\beta f(\bar x_j)}$. By the chain rule and Lemma~\ref{lem:derivative-of-J-proof}, wherever $\nabla f(x_k)\ne0$ one has $g'(f(x_k))=\prefactor{k}{\xx}$; by continuity of both sides in $f(x_k)$, $g'(f(\bar x_k))=\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}$. Writing $g'(r)=n\omega(r)[1-\beta(r-g(r)/n)]$ with $\omega(r)=e^{-\beta r}/(e^{-\beta r}+C_0)\in(0,1)$, a direct computation shows that at any critical point $r_0$ of $g$ (where the bracket vanishes), $g''(r_0)=-n\beta\omega(r_0)<0$.

    \emph{Step 1: $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}\ne0$.} Suppose $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}=0$. By the above, $r_0=f(\bar x_k)$ is a critical point of $g$, and it is a strict local maximum. If $f$ is non-constant near $\bar x_k$, there are points $y$ arbitrarily close to $\bar x_k$ with $f(y)$ close to but different from $r_0$, whence $nJ_\beta(\dots,y,\dots)=g(f(y))<g(r_0)=nJ_\beta(\bar\xx)$, contradicting local minimality. If $f$ is constant near $\bar x_k$, moving $x_k$ within that level set leaves every objective value, Gibbs weight, and gradient unchanged, producing a continuum of equilibria arbitrarily close to $\bar\xx$ and contradicting isolation. Hence $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}\ne0$.

    \emph{Step 2: $\nabla f(\bar x_k)=0$.} Since $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}\ne0$, $g'(f(\bar x_k))\ne0$, so $g$ is strictly monotone near $f(\bar x_k)$. If $\nabla f(\bar x_k)\ne0$, then $f$ takes values on both sides of $f(\bar x_k)$ arbitrarily near $\bar x_k$; strict monotonicity of $g$ then yields nearby configurations with $nJ_\beta<nJ_\beta(\bar\xx)$, contradicting local minimality. Hence $\nabla f(\bar x_k)=0$.

    \emph{Step 3: classification.} If $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}>0$, then $g$ is strictly increasing near $f(\bar x_k)$, so local minimality of $nJ_\beta$ forces $f(x_k)\ge f(\bar x_k)$ for $x_k$ near $\bar x_k$; thus $\bar x_k$ is a local minimum of $f$. If $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}<0$, the symmetric argument shows $\bar x_k$ is a local maximum. Because $nw_k>0$, the sign of $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}$ is that of $1-\beta(f(\bar x_k)-J_\beta(\bar\xx))$, giving the stated threshold. Finally, $\sum_k\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}=n$ forces some $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}>0$, so at least one particle is at a local minimum of $f$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:ensemble-min}]
    Fix $t$ and let $i$ attain the minimum, $f(x_i(t))=m(t)$. Since $f(x_i(t))\le f(x_j(t))$ for all $j$, we have $e^{-\beta_tf(x_j(t))}\le e^{-\beta_tf(x_i(t))}$, hence $\sum_je^{-\beta_tf(x_j(t))}\le ne^{-\beta_tf(x_i(t))}$ and therefore $nw_i(t)\ge1$. As $J_{\beta_t}(\xx(t))$ is a convex combination of the values $f(x_j(t))$, it satisfies $J_{\beta_t}(\xx(t))\ge f(x_i(t))$, so $1-\beta_t(f(x_i(t))-J_{\beta_t}(\xx(t)))\ge1$. Multiplying,
    \begin{equation}
        \prefactor{i}{\xx(t)}\ge1 \qquad \text{for every } i \text{ attaining the minimum at } t.
        \label{eq:ai-ge-1}
    \end{equation}
    Along such a particle,
    \[
        \frac{d}{dt}f(x_i(t)) = \langle\nabla f(x_i(t)),\dot x_i(t)\rangle = -\prefactor{i}{\xx(t)}\|\nabla f(x_i(t))\|^2 \le -\|\nabla f(x_i(t))\|^2.
    \]

    Consider $m=\min_k g_k$ with $g_k(t)=f(x_k(t))$. Since $\beta_t$ is only assumed measurable, each $x_k$ is a Carath\'eodory solution and hence absolutely continuous; as $f$ has locally Lipschitz gradient, each $g_k$ is absolutely continuous on compact time intervals, and a pointwise minimum of finitely many absolutely continuous functions is absolutely continuous. Therefore $m$ is absolutely continuous, and $m$ together with every $g_k$ is differentiable at a.e.\ $t$. At any such $t$, choose $i$ attaining the minimum; the function $g_i-m\ge0$ is then differentiable at $t$ and vanishes there, so it has zero derivative at $t$, giving $m'(t)=g_i'(t)$. Combined with the previous display,
    \[
        m'(t)=-\prefactor{i}{\xx(t)}\|\nabla f(x_i(t))\|^2\le-\|\nabla f(x_i(t))\|^2\le0 \qquad \text{for a.e. } t.
    \]
    Integrating, $m$ is nonincreasing, so every minimizing particle stays in $S_0=\{x:f(x)\le m(0)\}$, where the Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition (Definition~\ref{def:pl-condition}) applies:
    \[
        m'(t)\le-\|\nabla f(x_i(t))\|^2\le-2\mu\bigl(f(x_i(t))-f_\star\bigr)=-2\mu\bigl(m(t)-f_\star\bigr).
    \]
    Setting $y(t):=e^{2\mu t}(m(t)-f_\star)$, which is absolutely continuous, we get $y'(t)=e^{2\mu t}[m'(t)+2\mu(m(t)-f_\star)]\le0$ for a.e.\ $t$, so $y(t)\le y(0)$, i.e.
    \[
        m(t)-f_\star\le e^{-2\mu t}\bigl(m(0)-f_\star\bigr), \qquad t\ge0. \qedhere
    \]
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proofs of Section~\ref{sec:hitting-times}}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:channel-drift}]
    Write $r:=r(x)$. Factoring $e^{-\beta f(z_-)}$ out of the Gibbs weights at $\Gamma(x)$,
    \[
        w_1(\Gamma(x))=\frac{e^{-\beta r}}{n-1+e^{-\beta r}}, \qquad w_j(\Gamma(x))=\frac{1}{n-1+e^{-\beta r}} \quad (j\ge2),
    \]
    whence $J_\beta(\Gamma(x))-f(z_-)=re^{-\beta r}/(n-1+e^{-\beta r})=u(r)/n$, which is \eqref{eq:reduced-potential}. Therefore
    \[
        f(x)-J_\beta(\Gamma(x)) = r-\frac{re^{-\beta r}}{n-1+e^{-\beta r}} = \frac{(n-1)r}{n-1+e^{-\beta r}},
    \]
    and substituting into $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}=nw_1(\Gamma(x))[1-\beta(f(x)-J_\beta(\Gamma(x)))]$ gives
    \[
        \prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)} = \frac{ne^{-\beta r}}{n-1+e^{-\beta r}}\cdot\frac{n-1+e^{-\beta r}-\beta(n-1)r}{n-1+e^{-\beta r}} = \frac{ne^{-\beta r}q(r)}{(n-1+e^{-\beta r})^2}.
    \]
    Differentiating \eqref{eq:reduced-potential} directly yields the same expression for $u'(r)$, proving \eqref{eq:a1-equals-uprime}, consistently with the identity $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}=u'(r(x))$ already established in Lemma~\ref{lem:channel-invariant}. In particular $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}=0$ if and only if $q(r)=0$, which by the displayed formula for $f(x)-J_\beta$ is equivalent to $f(x)-J_\beta(\Gamma(x))=1/\beta$, i.e.\ to the vanishing of the Softmin threshold gap of the active particle.

    Since $q'(r)=-\beta(n-1)-\beta e^{-\beta r}<0$ for all $r\in\R$, $q(0)=n>0$, and $q(r)\to-\infty$ as $r\to\infty$, $q$ has a unique zero $r_c>0$. From $q(1/\beta)=e^{-1}>0$ we get $r_c>1/\beta$; and $q(r_c)=0$ reads $(n-1)(\beta r_c-1)=e^{-\beta r_c}<1$, giving $\beta r_c<n/(n-1)$. This is \eqref{eq:rc-location}. As $ne^{-\beta r}/(n-1+e^{-\beta r})^2>0$, the sign of $u'$ is the sign of $q$, so $u$ is strictly increasing on $(-\infty,r_c]$ and strictly decreasing on $[r_c,\infty)$.
\end{proof}


\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time}]
    \emph{Part 1.} For $r\le0$ we have $u(r)\le0$. For $r>0$, since $n-1+e^{-\beta r}>n-1$,
    \[
        u(r)<\frac{n}{n-1}re^{-\beta r}\le\frac{n}{n-1}\max_{\rho\ge0}\rho e^{-\beta\rho}=\frac{n}{e(n-1)\beta},
    \]
    the maximum being attained at $\rho=1/\beta$. Hence $u_c=\max_\R u<n/(e(n-1)\beta)$, which is \eqref{eq:barrier-cap}; the bound on $r_c$ is \eqref{eq:rc-location}.

    \emph{Part 2.} A channel path has the form $\phi(t)=\Gamma(\psi(t))$; the frozen coordinates stay at the critical point $z_-$, so their drift vanishes and, with $W(x):=u(r(x))$, the action reduces to $I_{0T}=\frac14\int_0^T|\dot\psi+W'(\psi)|^2\dd t$. The one-dimensional version of \eqref{eq:gradient-decomposition} is
    \[
        I_{0T}=W(\psi(T))-W(\psi(0))+\frac14\int_0^T|\dot\psi-W'(\psi)|^2\dd t.
    \]
    By \eqref{eq:adjacent-well-monotonicity}, $r$ is a strictly increasing bijection from $[z_-,s]$ onto $[0,h]$, so there is a unique $x_m\in(z_-,s]$ with $r(x_m)=\min\{h,r_c\}$. On $[z_-,x_m]$ we have $r(x)\le r_c$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:channel-drift} $W$ is strictly increasing there, with total rise $W(x_m)-W(z_-)=u_h$; on $[x_m,s]$ we have $r(x)\ge r_c$, so $W$ is nonincreasing. Every continuous channel path from $z_-$ to $s$ passes through $x_m$, and applying the displayed decomposition to the sub-path up to the first hitting time of $x_m$ bounds the action from below by $u_h$. This value is attained in the quasipotential sense by following the reversed deterministic flow from $z_-$ up to $x_m$ (cost exactly $u_h$) and then the forward flow toward $s$ at zero additional cost, with the standard finite-time approximations near the critical points. Therefore the channel quasipotential equals $u_h$, and since $V_\beta$ is an infimum over the larger class of all paths in $\R^n$,
    \[
        V_\beta(\Gamma(z_-),\Gamma(s))\le u_h.
    \]
    Because $u(r)/r=ne^{-\beta r}/(n-1+e^{-\beta r})<1$ for $r>0$, we have $u_h<\min\{h,r_c\}\le h$, which gives \eqref{eq:initial-bound}.

    \emph{Part 3.} Assume $r_c<h$. At $\Gamma(s)$ every particle sits at a critical point of $f$ (either $s$ or $z_-$). For the active particle, \eqref{eq:a1-equals-uprime} gives $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(s)}=u'(h)<0$ because $h>r_c$, and $f''(s)<0$ since $s$ is a nondegenerate maximum. For a frozen particle at $z_-$,
    \[
        \prefactor{j}{\Gamma(s)} = nw_j(\Gamma(s))\bigl[1+\beta\bigl(J_\beta(\Gamma(s))-f(z_-)\bigr)\bigr]>0
    \]
    by \eqref{eq:reduced-potential}, since $J_\beta(\Gamma(s))-f(z_-)=u(h)/n>0$, and $f''(z_-)>0$. Hence Corollary~\ref{cor:stability-sufficient} applies and $\Gamma(s)$ is a strict local minimum of $nJ_\beta$. On the $z_-$ side, $r$ decreases strictly from $h$ through $r_c$ as $x$ moves from $s$ toward $z_-$ (recall $x_m\in(z_-,s)$ is where $r(x_m)=r_c$, well-defined since $r_c<h$), so the channel well at $\Gamma(s)$ has barrier $u_c-u(h)$ on this side unconditionally --- this uses only $r_c<h$, not $\rho_+$.

    The $z_+$ side depends on $\rho_+$. On $(s,z_+)$, $r$ decreases strictly from $h$ to $\rho_+$ by \eqref{eq:adjacent-well-monotonicity}--\eqref{eq:relative-energy}.

    If $\rho_+<r_c$, then $r$ crosses $r_c$ on this interval, so there is a unique $\xi_+$ with $r(\xi_+)=r_c$. Between $s$ and $\xi_+$ the reduced potential rises from $u(h)$ to $u_c$; the gradient-action argument of Part 2 gives channel action $u_c-u(h)$, and since $0<u(h)<u_c$ we obtain \eqref{eq:post-saddle-bound}, so the well has matching barrier heights on both sides. Finally, for $x\in(\xi_+,z_+)$ we have $r(x)<r_c$, hence $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}=u'(r(x))>0$ by \eqref{eq:a1-equals-uprime}, while $f'(x)<0$; therefore the active coordinate of the deterministic channel flow satisfies $\dot x=-\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}f'(x)>0$, driving the particle toward $z_+$. Concatenating the admissible path of Part 2 (from $\Gamma(z_-)$ to $\Gamma(s)$, action $u_h$) with this one (from $\Gamma(s)$ to $\Gamma(\xi_+)$, action $u_c-u(h)$) gives an admissible path from $\Gamma(z_-)$ to $\Gamma(\xi_+)$ of total action $u_h+(u_c-u(h))$, proving \eqref{eq:two-stage-bound}.

    If instead $\rho_+\ge r_c$, then $r(x)\in[r_c,h)$ for every $x\in(s,z_+)$: the interval lies entirely on the decreasing side of $u$, where $u$ is strictly decreasing in $r$ while $r$ is strictly decreasing in $x$, so $u(r(x))$ is strictly increasing in $x$ throughout $(s,z_+)$, from $u(h)$ to $u(\rho_+)$. No interior peak occurs, so no point $\xi_+$ with $r(\xi_+)=r_c$ exists in $(s,z_+)$: the channel potential rises monotonically to $\Gamma(z_+)$ with no intermediate barrier to cross, and the total rise $u(\rho_+)-u(h)$ is at most the $z_-$-side barrier $u_c-u(h)$, since $u(\rho_+)\le u_c$. In this regime $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(z_+)}=u'(\rho_+)\le0$ while $f''(z_+)>0$, so by Theorem~\ref{thm:stationary-points} $\Gamma(z_+)$ is not itself a stable equilibrium, and no post-saddle well forms on this side.

    If instead $h\le r_c$, then $r(x)\le h\le r_c$ for $x$ near $s$, with equality only at $x=s$, and the strict monotonicity of $u$ on $(-\infty,r_c]$ shows that $x\mapsto u(r(x))$ has a strict local maximum at $s$. Hence $\Gamma(s)$ is not a local minimum of $nJ_\beta$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Hyperparameter Selection Methodology}
\label{app:hyperparameter-methodology}

Each of the 100 independent runs (Table~\ref{tab:general_settings}) is indexed by an integer $\text{seed}\in\{0,\dots,99\}$, and every worker seeds NumPy's global RNG once via \texttt{np.random.seed(seed*1000)} before drawing that run's initial swarm and simulating every algorithm on it. This seed indexing is reused identically at every sweep point (every $\delta$, $A$, or $d$) rather than redrawn independently per point: \texttt{seed=7} at $\delta=5$ and \texttt{seed=7} at $\delta=25$ consume the same underlying draw from the same seeded RNG state, remapped only by that point's own initialization bounds. Consequently the 100-run samples at different sweep points are correlated, not independent, and the quartile error bars in Figure~\ref{fig:well-entry-time} should be interpreted as within-seed variability across the ensemble rather than as independent per-point confidence intervals.

Two conventions govern every entry-time figure in this paper and are worth stating explicitly, since both affect how the curves should be read. First, a run that never reaches the target returns the timeout sentinel (max steps $\times\,\eta$) rather than a genuine entry time, so each plotted point is the mean over only those seeds whose logged status is \texttt{target\_reached}. A method's plotted curve is therefore conditional on success, and must be read together with its reached-fraction: where a baseline is faster than Softmin at a difficulty it only clears in a fraction of seeds, that mean is taken over the easier subset of the shared seed set, not over a comparable sample. Second, a point whose reached-fraction falls below $50\%$ is dropped from the plot entirely, to avoid a handful of lucky seeds rendering as a confident-looking point; points between $50\%$ and $90\%$ are plotted but annotated with the exact fraction. A curve that simply stops therefore marks the difficulty at which that method became unreliable, not the end of the sweep.

Per-algorithm settings are collected in Tables~\ref{tab:softmin_hyperparams}--\ref{tab:cbo_hyperparams}. Learning rates are unified to $\eta=0.001$ across every benchmark and algorithm (the Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} verification drivers excepted, which use their own, separately-motivated small-noise/small-step-size regime). The Softmin inverse temperature $\beta$ is selected per benchmark family via a two-stage, code-audited procedure (\texttt{src/hyperparameter\_diagnostics.py}), evaluated at a representative \emph{middle}-of-sweep setting rather than the hardest one, so the choice is not fit to the one condition the sweep is meant to stress-test: (1) an initial screen measures the distribution of $\beta(f_i-J_\beta)$ across the ensemble, flagging candidate values where trailing particles are not uniformly driven into the exponentially-suppressed regime; (2) because that screen alone favors values that are too small -- it only measures whether every particle receives rescue-drift, not what the reported first-entry-time metric rewards, namely the single fastest particle reaching the target, which benefits from a \emph{concentrated} ensemble via the leading particle's pre-factor approaching $n$ -- the candidates are then validated directly by measuring actual entry time. Across every benchmark tested (1D double well, 2D quadruple well, high-dimensional Ackley, and a spot check on Lennard-Jones), this second step confirmed $\beta=10$ as at or near the empirical optimum, matching the value used throughout; going lower is consistently slower or fails to reach the target at all, and going higher does not help (and is markedly worse on the quadruple well). The same direct-validation procedure was applied to Simulated Annealing's geometric cooling rate, and to comparing $\beta$ held fixed against both a decaying and an increasing schedule -- see Remark~\ref{rem:higher-dim} for the latter result. The Lennard-Jones benchmark additionally clips the gradient's Lennard-Jones term (never the confinement term, whose restoring force must scale freely to remain able to pull back an atom that has drifted outside the container) to a per-atom norm of 1.0 before the swarm's discrete update, applied identically to every algorithm; this clip is not applied to the L-BFGS-B local polish (``quench'') of each method's final configuration, which uses the true, unclipped gradient throughout, since a quasi-Newton method's curvature estimate is only valid for the true gradient. The transformer benchmark likewise applies a per-particle gradient-norm clip, at $10$, again identically for every algorithm; that value is selected in Table~\ref{tab:transformer_calibration} and is loose enough to reproduce the unclipped loss exactly, so it bounds a pathological step without shaping the dynamics.

\paragraph{Baseline calibration.} Simulated Annealing, CBO, and PSO were each given the same diagnostic-guided procedure as Softmin's $\beta$ and, where a comparable parameterization exists, the same candidate budget (Table~\ref{tab:calibration_budget}), rather than literature-standard values adopted without checking. The three cases are reported below; two of them end in negative results, which we report as such.

\paragraph{Simulated Annealing.} Seven candidate cooling rates were derived from target cooling \emph{timescales} $t^\star\in\{10^3,3{\times}10^3,10^4,3{\times}10^4,10^5,3{\times}10^5,10^6\}$ -- the number of steps to reach $5\%$ of the initial temperature, $\text{rate}=0.05^{1/t^\star}$ -- rather than hand-picked decimals, and validated by direct entry-time measurement in the same way as $\beta$; the rate $0.99995$ was confirmed on both wells. The initial temperature $T_0$ is inherently problem-scale-dependent, so no universal literature default exists, and it was calibrated separately. Escape time is a degenerate criterion for $T_0$ in this continuous, always-accept diffusion, since larger $T_0$ is monotonically faster with no interior optimum and eventually indistinguishable from bounded random search; we therefore used a Kramers/Arrhenius criterion instead. Five candidates $T_0=c\,h$ with $c\in\{0.25,0.5,1,2,4\}$, where $h$ is the double well's mid-sweep barrier height, were scored by the closed-form escape factor $\exp(-h/T_0)$, and we selected the value placing that factor in the \emph{low} band $[0.01,0.05]$: a genuine, non-trivial barrier, rather than the near-unity band a literal reading of the discrete-Metropolis initial-acceptance-ratio heuristic \cite{kirkpatrick1983optimization} would suggest, which would leave the barrier thermodynamically negligible from $t=0$ and trivialize precisely the obstacle this baseline is meant to face. The selected $T_0=h/4$ reached the target within a few hundred steps at the calibration midpoint and transferred to the quadruple well. It nonetheless fails at the hardest points of the double-well sweep ($h\gtrsim156$). That failure is a property of the cooling schedule rather than of the step budget: with early stopping disabled and a horizon of $1{,}500{,}000$ steps, fifteen times the original probe, the best value found is unchanged from step $10{,}000$ onward at both $h=156.1$ and $h=224.8$, consistent with the geometric cooling driving the temperature to $\approx0$ well before step $200{,}000$ and freezing the dynamics. Applying the same criterion to Ackley's own barrier scale was inconclusive even at a five-times-longer diagnostic budget, so Ackley and Lennard-Jones retain $T_0=10$.

\paragraph{CBO.} The same 11-value $\beta$ grid used for Softmin was swept, together with two annealed-schedule variants (growth-only, matching CBO's own literature convention rather than Softmin's decaying schedule). Every candidate times out through consensus collapse on the double well, the quadruple well, \emph{and} Ackley, at the identical step regardless of which $\beta$ was used: direct testing across $\beta\in\{0.01,0.02,0.1,1.0,10.0\}$ produced position collapse at the same step on every benchmark tested. The collapse is therefore a $\beta$-independent structural property of vanilla CBO's self-shrinking anisotropic diffusion, not a symptom of a poorly chosen $\beta$. Since the choice is empirically immaterial, we report $\beta=0.01$, which lies in CBO's literature-typical range.

\paragraph{PSO.} PSO has no single temperature-like scalar to sweep, so six named, literature-grounded $(w,c_1,c_2)$ triples were tested instead: the canonical Clerc--Kennedy constriction coefficients \cite{clerc2002particle}; the original Kennedy--Eberhart rule \cite{kennedy1995particle}; both endpoints of the Shi--Eberhart linearly-decreasing-inertia schedule \cite{shi1998modified}; a social-weighted variant biased toward the swarm's global best; and a deliberately non-contractive stress-test triple. All six were pre-filtered by a finite/at-bound stability probe. On the double well and quadruple well, PSO shows the same negative result as CBO. On Ackley, by contrast, the literature-standard constriction coefficients themselves \emph{fail}, timing out through consensus collapse at every dimension probed ($d=50,70,90$), while two of the six alternatives succeed at all three; the faster of those, $(w,c_1,c_2)=(0.4,2.0,2.0)$, was adopted for Ackley and is used both in Section~\ref{sec:ackley-benchmark} and for the Ackley-$d{=}50$ ablation in Appendix~\ref{apx:ablation-study}. A spot check found it also improves the Lennard-Jones quenched result, so it is used there too (Table~\ref{tab:general_settings}).

\subsection{Experimental Details}
\label{apx:experiment-details}

This appendix gives, for each benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:numerical-experiments}, the precise
setup, reporting conventions and detailed numbers behind the summary in the main text.

\subsubsection{Protocol and Reporting Conventions}
\label{apx:protocol}

We evaluate the capacity of Softmin dynamics to escape local minima and outrun sub-optimal basins by comparing fixed-$\beta$ ($\beta=10$) Softmin against seven baselines: Simulated Annealing (SA) \cite{gelfand1990sampling}; overdamped Langevin dynamics \cite{welling2011bayesian}, a control that shares Softmin's step size and noise level but has no interaction; Gradient-Aware Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO) \cite{carrillo2021consensus}, with and without upward $\beta$-annealing; Gradient-Enhanced Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) \cite{noel2012new}; and the canonical, gradient-free formulations of both CBO \cite{CarrilloChoiTotzeckTse2018, pinnau2017consensus} and PSO \cite{kennedy1995particle}. The ensemble-size sweep compares Softmin with Simulated Annealing alone, and the transformer benchmark with Simulated Annealing, Langevin dynamics, Adam and SGD. The continuous dynamics are discretized using the Euler--Maruyama scheme with step size $\eta$, and every entry time we report is in continuous-time units, that is (number of steps) $\times\,\eta$, except on the transformer benchmark, where the methods use different step sizes and entry time is reported in steps. Entry time is measured against a value-space target: for the wells, $0.15$ of that benchmark's own depth gap above the target well's value, so the tolerance scales with the sweep instead of being fixed in absolute terms; for Lennard-Jones, a tolerance of $10^{-2}$ above the literature global minimum; for Ackley, the absolute value $f\le1$; and for the transformer, a training loss of $0.10$. Each reported entry-time mean is taken over those seeds that actually reached the target, and any sweep point at which fewer than half the seeds reach it is omitted from the entry-time panels rather than plotted from a small surviving sample. Because that convention makes an unreliable method disappear rather than degrade, every entry-time result is accompanied by the reached-fraction at every swept point, with no cutoff and no masking, as a reliability panel or, for Ackley, in the caption of Figure~\ref{fig:ackley-experiments} and the text of Appendix~\ref{apx:ackley}, so a method's speed is never shown without its reliability. Landscape formulations, hyperparameters, initialization bounds, hardware specifications, and real per-particle gradient/objective evaluation counts and wall-clock time are detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparameters}; the source code is available at \cite{softmin_energy_2026}.

One caveat frames everything that follows. Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} is a $\sigma\downarrow0$ statement, whereas the analytic benchmarks below run at $\sigma=1$ (the transformer benchmark uses $\sigma=10^{-5}$, see Appendix~\ref{apx:transformer}): at $\beta=10$ and $n=100$ the bound of \eqref{eq:barrier-cap} is $0.0372$ and the channel maximum it bounds is $u_c\approx0.0370$, so $u_c/\sigma\approx0.04$ and the reduced landscape along the channel is essentially flat relative to the noise. The large-deviation exponent therefore carries little information at the noise level used here, and these experiments should be read as testing whether the Softmin coupling helps in a practically relevant regime, not as a numerical confirmation of the exponent. The small-noise regime the theorem does describe is probed separately in Appendix~\ref{apx:theory-verification}, where $\sigma$ is swept toward $0$.

\subsubsection{Double-Well and Quadruple-Well Benchmarks}
\label{apx:wells}

Both well families are swept through the quadratic coefficient of their defining quartic (Table~\ref{tab:general_settings}): $\delta$ for the 1D double well $f(x)=x^4-\delta x^2+0.05x$, and $A$ for the 2D quadruple well $f(x,y)=x^4+y^4-A(x^2+y^2)+xy$. We plot each sweep against the quantity that actually sets its difficulty rather than against the raw coefficient. For the double well this is the barrier height $h=f(s)-f(z_-)$, which grows like $\delta^2/4$, from $h=0.2$ at $\delta=1$ to $h=224.8$ at $\delta=30$; for the quadruple well it is the depth gap between the shallow diagonal minimum and the deep anti-diagonal one, which equals $A$ exactly (Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparameter-methodology}).

We first check that simulations on a 1D double well, at the small noise the theorem requires, are consistent with the large deviation scaling of Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time}; results are in Appendix~\ref{apx:other-experiments} (Figures~\ref{fig:theory} and \ref{fig:theory-sensitivity}), alongside the ascent-descent mechanism ablation (Appendix~\ref{apx:ablation-study}), the per-particle pre-factor illustration (Figure~\ref{fig:ascent-descent}), and the full cost comparison (Table~\ref{tab:cost_comparison}).

Figure~\ref{fig:experiments} (top left) reports reliability across barrier heights $h$. Softmin reaches the target in all 100 seeds at every $h$ tested, across a sweep spanning a factor of about $1100$ in barrier height ($h=0.2$ to $h=224.8$). Every baseline eventually fails. SA holds at 100\% while the barrier is small, then degrades as it grows: 86\% at $h=99.8$, 1\% at $h=156.1$, and 0\% at $h=224.8$. Langevin fails far earlier, already at 0\% by $h=24.9$ having succeeded at $h=6.2$. CBO and PSO, gradient-aware and canonical alike, reach the target in at most 3\% of seeds at any barrier height tested, far below the 50\% floor a point must clear to be plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:well-entry-time} (Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparameter-methodology}), and so never appear there.

This separation is the qualitative signature Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} describes. A method whose quasipotential barrier is capped at order $1/\beta$ \emph{independently of the true barrier height} should keep escaping as $h$ grows, while a method governed by the true exponent $h$ -- SA and Langevin both -- should fail once $h$ is large enough, which is the pattern the top row shows. Softmin's entry time (Figure~\ref{fig:well-entry-time}, left) quantifies the same behavior from the other side: its mean grows from $0.12$ at $h=0.2$ to $13.2$ at $h=224.8$, a factor of about $110$ against that factor of about $1100$ in barrier height, that is, far more slowly than the barrier itself, whereas the escape time of a method governed by the true barrier grows exponentially in it. We put this no more strongly than consistency, for the reason given at the start of Section~\ref{sec:numerical-experiments}: at $\sigma=1$ the proven exponent is far too small to be the operative effect, and the ablation of Appendix~\ref{apx:ablation-study} finds the Gibbs weighting the more plausible source of the advantage at $\beta=10$. What the theory predicts and the data show is the same \emph{shape}, not an established causal link.

SA is genuinely faster than Softmin wherever it still succeeds, and Figure~\ref{fig:well-entry-time} should not be read as saying otherwise: at the mid-sweep point $h=56.1$ SA reaches the target in a mean $0.22$ against Softmin's $3.67$. That comparison is conditional on success, however, and averages over only the seeds each method solved. Table~\ref{tab:cost_to_solution} prices the same runs without conditioning.

Figure~\ref{fig:experiments} (right column) extends this to the 2D quadruple well, whose \texttt{particle\_init} and target sit at different depths, with a depth gap equal to $A$ exactly (Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparameter-methodology}, Table~\ref{tab:general_settings}). The same pattern holds and sharpens. Softmin again reaches the target at every depth gap tested (2 through 30, 100\% of seeds throughout), while SA's reached-fraction falls to 17\% by $A=25$ and 0\% by $A=30$, and Langevin fails already by $A=10$ (0\%, having succeeded at $A=5$). CBO and PSO reach the target in at most 3\% of seeds at every depth gap tested here; an independent, algorithm-agnostic stagnation check, logged per run (Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparameter-methodology}), confirms this is a genuine stall rather than an unreached timeout. As on the double well, SA is faster where it still succeeds ($0.15$ mean entry time at $A=15$ against Softmin's $0.26$), on the seeds it solved.

\paragraph{Cost to solution.} Entry time counts steps, not the price of a solution. A practitioner cannot know in advance which instances a method will solve, so the cost that matters is the expected cost of obtaining a success, including the runs that produce none. We therefore report the expected running time (ERT) standard in black-box optimization benchmarking \cite{hansen2016coco}: the total cost over all 100 seeds divided by the number of successes, which under a restart model is the expected cost of the first success, and is undefined (infinite) where a method never succeeds. Table~\ref{tab:cost_to_solution} gives it in wall-clock seconds along the double-well sweep. The bottom row of Figure~\ref{fig:experiments} gives it at every sweep point of both benchmarks and for every method, measured instead in objective plus gradient evaluations: this unit does not depend on the hardware, and unlike gradient evaluations alone it also charges the canonical, gradient-free CBO and PSO, which use none. A gap marks a point at which no seed succeeded, so that the expected cost is infinite, and an isolated marker a point at which only a few did; the CBO and PSO markers at low barriers, and Simulated Annealing's single marker near $h=210$, are of this kind. In this unit Simulated Annealing's expected cost first exceeds Softmin's at $h=90.1$ on the double well, consistent with the crossover between $h=56.1$ and $h=99.8$ in the table, and at a depth gap of $21$ on the quadruple well.

Read this way the trade-off is a crossover, not a ranking. Where the barrier is small SA is the cheaper method outright, by roughly an order of magnitude, and we do not claim otherwise; averaged over only the runs it solves, across both well sweeps, SA costs $0.79$ s against Softmin's $11.68$ s. What changes past the crossover is not that SA becomes slower but that its cost stops being bounded at all: its geometric cooling drives the temperature to $\approx0$ after a fixed number of steps regardless of the barrier actually faced, so it freezes rather than slows, and additional budget does not recover it (Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparameter-methodology} verifies this directly at $1{,}500{,}000$ steps, fifteen times the original probe, with the best value unchanged from step $10{,}000$ onward). Softmin's ERT meanwhile grows by a factor of $19$ between $h=6.2$ and $h=224.8$, a factor of $36$ in barrier height, and by a factor of about $110$ in evaluations over the full sweep (Figure~\ref{fig:experiments}), which spans a factor of about $1100$. Aggregated over the full double-well and quadruple-well sweep the same asymmetry appears as a single number: Softmin's mean wall-clock is $11.68$ s against SA's $351.57$ s and Langevin's $1033.74$ s, a $30\times$ and $88\times$ difference, rising to $42\times$ and $384\times$ in ERT (Table~\ref{tab:cost_comparison}). Softmin's three wall-clock figures coincide in the same comparison because it never times out.

\subsubsection{Lennard-Jones Cluster Benchmark}
\label{apx:lj}

Lennard-Jones clusters are our first benchmark for scaling with dimension: $N$ atoms interact through the pairwise potential $V(r)=4\epsilon\bigl[(r_0/r)^{12}-(r_0/r)^6\bigr]$ \cite{jones1924determination}, giving a search space of dimension $d=3N$ whose number of local minima grows exponentially in $N$. Here $r$ is the interatomic distance and $r_0$ the length scale, conventionally written $\sigma$; we rename it because $\sigma$ denotes the noise intensity throughout this paper. We work in reduced units, $\epsilon=r_0=1$. We use the $N\in\{13,38,55,75\}$ clusters ($d$ up to $225$) from the Cambridge Cluster Database \cite{wales1997global}, whose lowest known energies serve as the literature global minimum against which every method is scored. Following the standard basin-hopping convention of separating global search from local relaxation \cite{wales1997global}, each method's final configuration is locally polished, or \emph{quenched}, via L-BFGS-B to its nearest local minimum before scoring; we report this quenched configuration's energy gap above the literature global minimum, rather than the raw configuration's, so the comparison reflects basin-finding rather than residual local-convergence noise. The strict hit-time target used for the double-well and quadruple-well benchmarks above is rarely reached by any method (Softmin included) beyond the smallest cluster size, once $N\geq38$, so the quenched-energy gap is the informative comparison at this scale.

Figure~\ref{fig:lj-experiments} reports the quenched-energy gap. Softmin's gap stays small and essentially flat across the full range tested (from $0$ at $N=13$ to $3.7$ at $N=75$), while every baseline's gap grows roughly linearly with $N$, from $1.8$--$5.6$ at $N=13$ to $23$--$30$ at $N=75$. Softmin's advantage over every baseline \emph{widens}, not narrows, as the cluster grows: the opposite of the ordinary curse-of-dimensionality intuition.

\subsubsection{High-Dimensional Ackley Benchmark}
\label{apx:ackley}

Our final benchmark is the generalized Ackley function in $d\in\{10,20,\dots,90\}$ dimensions, which superimposes a dense lattice of shallow local minima, generated by a cosine term, on a single smooth radial funnel. Both of its terms are per-coordinate averages, so its global minimum is $0$ at the origin for every $d$ and the hit-time target can be the fixed absolute value $f\le1$ with no dimension-dependent rescaling. This is the benchmark on which Softmin does worst, and Figure~\ref{fig:ackley-experiments} reports it in full.

Particle Swarm Optimization is the fastest method here at every dimension, by a wide margin. PSO reaches the target in a mean $0.03$ at $d=10$, rising gently to $1.47$ at $d=90$, while Softmin does not reach it at all within the step budget at $d=10$ or $d=20$ (0\% of seeds) and is far slower where it does succeed: $5055$ at $d=30$, $97.9$ at $d=50$, $8.98$ at $d=90$. Simulated Annealing succeeds only at $d=10$; CBO degrades from 71\% reached at $d=10$ to 5\% at $d=30$; Langevin and canonical CBO never reach the target at any dimension tested.

What separates the methods here, however, is not access to the gradient. The canonical gradient-free PSO is statistically indistinguishable from the gradient-enhanced form at every dimension, the two means agreeing to within $15\%$ throughout ($0.026$ vs.\ $0.027$ at $d=10$, $1.35$ vs.\ $1.47$ at $d=90$): at $\eta=0.001$ the gradient step contributes essentially nothing beside PSO's own velocity update. The split is instead between the PSO family and everything else. Outside that family, Softmin is the only method that becomes reliable as the dimension grows, reaching the target in every run from $d=40$ on after failing entirely at $d=10$ and $d=20$: Simulated Annealing drops to $0\%$ at $d=20$, both gradient-aware CBO variants fall below the $50\%$ reliability floor there and reach $0\%$ by $d=50$, and Langevin and canonical CBO never succeed at any dimension tested. Ackley therefore discriminates search dynamics rather than gradient information, and is a weak test of the question this paper is about.

Read against dimension, moreover, the trend runs in Softmin's favor and tracks the theory. The barrier out of the global basin along a single coordinate direction, $\max_{t\in[0,1]}f(te_1)-f(0)$, falls monotonically as $d$ grows -- from $1.23$ at $d=10$ to $0.42$ at $d=90$ -- because both terms of Ackley are per-coordinate averages. Softmin's entry time falls with it, and the gap to PSO closes by nearly four orders of magnitude across the sweep, from a factor of about $3.6\times10^4$ at $d=30$ to a factor of $6$ at $d=90$. These barriers remain well above the switching level $r_c=0.100$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:channel-drift} at $\beta=10$, by a factor of $4$ to $12$, so the sign-reversal mechanism is active throughout, and Softmin's improvement is consistent with the barrier flattening of Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} rather than with the mechanism lapsing. We put this no more strongly than consistency: at the $\sigma=1$ used here the proven exponent is too small to be the operative effect, as noted at the start of Section~\ref{sec:numerical-experiments}, and agreement between two monotone trends is not on its own evidence of a causal link. Reliability moves the same way: Softmin's reached-fraction rises to $100\%$ from $d=40$ onward, so its $d=10$ and $d=20$ failures are plausibly the continuation of that trend against a fixed budget rather than a separate effect, though we did not confirm this by raising the budget, and at $d=90$ Softmin is the most reliable method in the comparison at $100\%$, against $96\%$ for gradient-enhanced PSO, $58\%$ for the canonical form, and $0\%$ for everything else.

None of this overturns the headline: PSO is faster than Softmin at every dimension tested, and we do not claim otherwise. What Ackley establishes is the boundary of the claim: on a landscape whose difficulty is carried by a dense lattice of shallow minima on a smooth global funnel, and on which gradient information demonstrably buys nothing, barrier flattening delivers reliability but not speed. We do not identify what does drive PSO's advantage here. That its velocity-based attraction toward the swarm's global best is well suited to a single smooth funnel is a natural conjecture, but we have not tested it and nothing in our experiments distinguishes it from competing explanations; we record it as an open question rather than a finding. Softmin's $\beta$ was fixed at the mid-sweep dimension $d=50$ by the same pre-committed protocol applied to every benchmark family and every baseline (Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparameter-methodology}), and never re-tuned per dimension; the $d=10$ and $d=20$ results are therefore genuinely out-of-sample. We note finally that the PSO coefficients used here are not the literature-standard constriction values, which themselves fail at every Ackley dimension probed ($d=50,70,90$); they were selected by the same diagnostic-guided procedure applied to every baseline (Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparameter-methodology}).

\subsubsection{Ensemble-Size Sweep}
\label{apx:particle-sweep}

Every benchmark above fixes the swarm at $n=100$ (Table~\ref{tab:general_settings}), so the
reliability result is so far a statement at a single ensemble size. This section varies $n$
directly, on the 2D quadruple well, at three depth gaps chosen to span Simulated Annealing's
collapse at $n=100$: $A=10$ and $A=20$, where it reaches the target in every seed, and $A=30$,
where it reaches it in none.

The comparison is deliberately restricted to Softmin and Simulated Annealing, and is sharper than
it first appears. Every method in this paper is run as an ensemble, so Simulated Annealing at
ensemble size $n$ is $n$ \emph{independent} annealed chains: increasing $n$ hands it $n$ restarts
of a single-chain method, and its reliability may well improve with $n$ for that reason alone. The
question this sweep asks is therefore not whether an ensemble helps, which it does for both
methods, but whether Softmin's \emph{coupling} buys anything beyond embarrassingly-parallel
restarts at matched cost. Both methods perform the same $O(n)$ work per step, so per-particle
evaluation counts are directly comparable at every $n$. Hyperparameters are held at the values
calibrated at $n=100$ for this benchmark and are not re-tuned per $n$, so $n=100$ is the in-sample
point and every other ensemble size is out-of-sample for both methods symmetrically.

\paragraph{Reading Figure~\ref{fig:particle-sweep}.} Ensemble-size sweep on the 2D quadruple well at depth gap $A=20$, $n$ from 5 to 400 (log axis), 100 seeds per point. This is the one difficulty at which the two methods separate as $n$ varies: at $A=10$ both reach every seed at every $n$, and at $A=30$ Simulated Annealing reaches essentially none at any $n$, so neither discriminates. Both are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:particle-sweep-all}. Left: reliability, the fraction of seeds reaching the value-space target, plotted at every $n$ with no cutoff; the dashed line is the independent-restart law $1-(1-p)^n$ with the single per-chain probability $p=0.074$ fitted from $n=5$ alone and every larger ensemble extrapolated, not fitted (Table~\ref{tab:restart_law}). Center and right are deliberately two different accountings of cost, and the contrast between them is the point. Center: mean first-entry time over the seeds that reached the target \emph{only}, on which Simulated Annealing lies below Softmin throughout -- where it works, it is genuinely the faster method, and we do not claim otherwise. Right: expected cost to solution (ERT), the total per-particle gradient work over \emph{all} seeds divided by the number of successes, which charges each method for the runs it never solves. On that accounting the order reverses below $n=100$: at $n=5$ Simulated Annealing's conditional cost is $1.1\times10^4$ against an ERT of $5.3\times10^6$, because $68\%$ of its runs fail and contribute nothing to the conditional average.

Softmin reaches the target in all $100$ seeds at every one of the $21$ (difficulty, ensemble size)
combinations, including at $n=5$, a twentieth of the ensemble used everywhere else in this paper.
Simulated Annealing splits sharply by difficulty, and the way it splits is the result of this
section. At $A=10$ it also reaches every seed at every $n$. At $A=20$ its reliability climbs with
$n$ exactly as independent restarts predict: fitting a single per-chain success probability from
the smallest ensemble alone, $p=1-(1-0.32)^{1/5}=0.074$, the restart law $1-(1-p)^n$ predicts
$0.54$, $0.85$, $0.98$ and $0.9996$ at $n=10,25,50,100$ against the measured $0.51$, $0.84$,
$0.98$ and $1.00$, and an exact binomial test rejects the law at no swept point (smallest
$p$-value $0.62$). The coupling in Softmin's interaction is what the ensemble buys that restarts
do not: for $n=5$ \emph{independent} chains to reach every seed as Softmin does would require a
per-chain probability of at least $0.49$, against the $0.074$ measured here. The comparison with
Langevin dynamics sharpens this further. With $n=1$ the pre-factor is identically $1$, so Softmin's
update reduces exactly to that of Langevin dynamics with the same $\eta$ and $\sigma$
(Table~\ref{tab:langevin_hyperparams}), which on the quadruple well reaches the target in no seed from
$A=10$ on even with $n=100$ independent particles (Appendix~\ref{apx:wells}). Five Softmin particles
thus differ from five independent copies of a method that fails at all three depth gaps only in their
interaction. At $A=30$ the same
law, fitted from the single success observed across $400\times100$ chains, gives
$p\approx2.5\times10^{-5}$, some $3000$ times smaller than at $A=20$, and no ensemble size tested
recovers it: Simulated Annealing reaches $0\%$ of seeds up to $n=200$ and $1\%$ at $n=400$, while
Softmin is at $100\%$ from $n=5$ on.

That contrast is the signature Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} predicts. Restarts multiply a
per-chain success probability without changing its exponent, so a mechanism whose escape rate is
governed by the true barrier $h$ can be rescued by more chains at a moderate barrier and cannot be
at a large one, while a mechanism whose effective barrier is capped independently of $h$ should not
need them at either. We put this no more strongly than consistency, for the reasons given at the start of Section~\ref{sec:numerical-experiments}: at $\sigma=1$ the proven cap is too small to be the operative effect, and an
agreement of this kind does not on its own establish the mechanism.

The ensemble is not free, and the two methods spend it on opposite things. Both perform the same
$O(n)$ work per step, so per-particle gradient counts are directly comparable between them at
every $n$; charging each method for its failures as well as its successes, as the expected running
time of Section~\ref{sec:double-quadruple-well} does -- the quantity plotted in the right panels of
Figures~\ref{fig:particle-sweep} and~\ref{fig:particle-sweep-all} -- Softmin is cheapest at its \emph{smallest}
ensemble at every difficulty ($1.5\times10^4$, $3.4\times10^4$ and $5.4\times10^4$ evaluations at
$A=10,20,30$ with $n=5$), because it does not need the extra particles for reliability and pays for
each one in gradient evaluations. Simulated Annealing's expected cost instead \emph{falls} with $n$
wherever its reliability is still climbing, from $5.3\times10^6$ at $n=5$ to $6.0\times10^4$ at
$n=100$ for $A=20$, because there the restarts are buying reliability it otherwise lacks. The
resulting comparison is difficulty-dependent in the same way as Section~\ref{sec:double-quadruple-well}:
at $A=10$ Simulated Annealing is cheaper at every $n$, by a factor of about $11$ at $n=5$; at
$A=20$ Softmin is some $155$ times cheaper at $n=5$, and once Simulated Annealing's reliability
saturates at $n\geq100$ the two sit within about a factor of two of each other in either direction;
at $A=30$ Softmin's cheapest configuration costs $5.4\times10^4$ evaluations while Simulated
Annealing's expected cost is infinite up to $n=200$ and $2.0\times10^{10}$ at $n=400$. None of this
contradicts the center panels of the two figures, where Simulated Annealing lies below Softmin at
every point of the sweep: that is cost on solved runs alone, and the reversal here is precisely the
price of the seeds it does not solve. Which of the two accountings is the relevant one depends on
whether a practitioner can tell in advance that a given instance will be solved; when they cannot,
it is the expected cost that they pay.

Two qualifications belong with this result. The first is that Simulated Annealing's disadvantage at
$A=20$ is recoverable by brute force: by $n=100$ it reaches every seed, and from there the two expected
costs are within about a factor of two of each other in either direction (Simulated Annealing's is
lower at $n=100$ and $n=400$, and higher at $n=200$), so what the sweep establishes at this
difficulty is a gap at small ensembles and not a permanent one
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The second concerns Softmin's own entry time at $A=30$, which is
not monotone in $n$. The median over solved seeds falls from $9.43$ at $n=25$ to $1.18$ at $n=50$
and rises again to $21.31$ at $n=100$, where the distribution also carries a heavy right tail
($24\%$ of solved seeds above $t=50$) that the mean in Figure~\ref{fig:particle-sweep-all} reports
as a spike. Because a whole-distribution shift of that size over $100$ seeds could still be an
unlucky draw, we re-ran the three affected ensemble sizes on a \emph{disjoint} set of $100$ seeds
with nothing else changed (\texttt{src/confirm\_particle\_anomaly.py}). It reproduces: the fresh
medians are $11.74$, $1.18$ and $23.70$, keeping the same shape across $n$, and a Mann--Whitney
test does not distinguish the two seed batches at any of the three points ($p=0.37$, $0.56$,
$0.51$). The effect is therefore a property of the ensemble size at this difficulty rather than a
sampling accident, and we have no explanation for it; we report it because it qualifies the cost
panels, and note that reliability is untouched -- Softmin reaches every seed at every ensemble size
swept, so the result this section is about does not rest on it.

\subsubsection{Transformer Benchmark}
\label{apx:transformer}

This appendix gives the setup of the transformer benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:transformer} and the
detailed analysis behind its summary.

\paragraph{Task.} The task is modular addition, $(a+b) \bmod p$ with $p=53$, presented to the model as the sequence
$[a, b, {=}]$ and predicted from the final position. This is the standard controlled testbed for
small transformers \cite{power2022grokking,nanda2023progress}, and, more directly relevant here, it
is already established as an instrument for \emph{comparing optimizers} on transformer training
\cite{tveit2025muon}, with its depth axis swept within the same task family
\cite{manir2026grokking}. Transformer training is a genuinely hard non-convex problem rather
than a stand-in for one \cite{ahn2023linear}, and small transformers are a recognized proxy for
optimization difficulties that appear at scale \cite{wortsman2023smallscale}. Two practical
properties matter for our purposes: the full dataset is small enough to take \emph{exact,
full-batch} gradients, so the comparison keeps the exact-gradient setting used throughout this
paper rather than introducing minibatch noise as a new confound; and the whole benchmark is
reproducible from a seed with no data download.

\paragraph{Depth sweep and baselines.} We sweep depth $\in\{2,5,10,20,50\}$ with $d_{\text{model}}$ and the head count held fixed, so
depth is the only architectural variable; the flattened parameter count $d$ is the optimization
dimension, from $1.07\times10^5$ at depth 2 to $2.51\times10^6$ at depth 50, some five to six orders
of magnitude above the well benchmarks and three to four above the largest Lennard-Jones cluster. The range is
deliberately wide. A shallower $\{1,2,4,6\}$ sweep did not produce a monotone difficulty gradient,
with Adam solving depth 2 faster than depth 1, so we extended depth by an order of magnitude to
test whether a deeper model would; as reported below, it does not. A feasibility probe
(\texttt{src/probe\_depth\_feasibility.py}) confirms the deep end is genuinely trainable rather
than a foregone failure: the measured gradient norm stays flat between $0.79$ and $0.93$ from
depth 5 to depth 50, neither exploding nor vanishing, and Adam drives the loss well below chance
at every depth tested. Alongside the swarm baselines we add Adam \cite{kingma2014adam} and SGD
with momentum, without which a neural-network comparison would not be a comparison. Crucially,
these are run as $n$ \emph{independent} chains exactly like every other baseline, so they receive
the same $n=10$ restarts and the same per-step budget the Softmin ensemble consumes: the
comparison is cost-matched rather than one-run-against-ten. All $n$ particles are evaluated in a
single batched forward and backward pass, since a Softmin step costs one objective and one
gradient evaluation per particle. Entry time is measured against a training-loss target of
$0.10$, as for Lennard-Jones, and reliability is again the fraction of seeds reaching it. Consensus-Based and
Particle Swarm Optimization are omitted here: they reach the target in at most $3\%$ of seeds at every
difficulty on both well benchmarks, so sweeping them across five depths would spend a substantial part
of the budget re-measuring a flat zero.

\paragraph{Calibration.} Three settings do not transfer to weight space and are calibrated for this benchmark family by the
same diagnostic-guided procedure applied to every other family
(Appendix~\ref{app:hyperparameter-methodology}, Table~\ref{tab:transformer_calibration}), exactly
as Simulated Annealing's $T_0$ and Particle Swarm Optimization's coefficients already are. The
first is the learning rate. At $\eta=10^{-3}$, the value used on the analytic benchmarks, a step
moves each coordinate by some $1.4\times10^{-6}$ against weights of scale $10^{-1}$, and the loss
does not leave its initial value within any feasible budget: after 400 steps it sits at $3.9996$,
\emph{above} the chance level $\log p=3.970$, at every gradient-clipping value tested. Adam is
exempt by construction, since it rescales per coordinate; that is precisely why it must not be the
only method able to move, and $\eta=0.1$ is selected instead. It remains stable at the deepest sweep point: the sweep runs the
adopted configuration unchanged at every depth, and every run reaches the target at depth 50 as at
every other depth.
The second is the gradient clip, which a first calibration pass on a 1-layer model had found
inactive and which does not stay inactive as depth grows: at depth 10 the per-particle gradient
norm averages $0.9311$ but reaches $1.0341$, so a clip of $1$ does bind, and it costs a great
deal -- at $\eta=0.1$ and 400 steps, clipping at $1$ reaches only $1.1648$ against $0.0754$ at
$10$. We therefore raise the clip to $10$, which reproduces the unclipped loss to four decimals
and so bounds a pathological step without shaping the dynamics. The third is the noise level. The per-step perturbation has standard deviation
$\sqrt{2\sigma\eta}$, so at $\eta=0.1$ the analytic benchmarks' $\sigma=1$ would inject about $0.45$
per coordinate, against weights of scale $10^{-1}$. Training breaks down long before that: already
$\sigma=10^{-3}$ (a perturbation of about $0.014$) leaves the loss at $3.95$, at chance, and
$\sigma=10^{-2}$ (about $0.045$) at $4.08$, above it. The sweep's own optimum is
$\sigma=0$, which we deliberately do not adopt: it would reduce Simulated Annealing and Langevin
dynamics to one and the same deterministic gradient descent, collapsing two of the four baselines
into duplicates of each other. We use $\sigma=10^{-5}$, the
smallest value that keeps every method's dynamics distinct, at a cost well inside the target
margin.

The inverse temperature is \emph{not} re-tuned: $\beta=10$ is carried over from every other
benchmark. The sweep is decisive about its order of magnitude, since $\beta=0.1$ and $\beta=1$ do
not learn here at all, but $\beta=10$ and $\beta=100$ both clear the target comfortably, so the
shared value is kept rather than the nominal per-benchmark optimum. Adam and SGD use their
literature-standard defaults, the corresponding convention for them.

\paragraph{Reliability and depth.} Every method reaches the target on every seed at every depth: all 125 runs succeed.
This benchmark therefore does not discriminate on
reliability, and we draw no reliability conclusion from it. Nor did depth produce the difficulty
gradient the sweep was designed around. Every baseline reaches the target \emph{sooner} in the
deepest model than in the shallowest: from depth 2 to depth 50 the mean entry time falls by a
factor of $2.48$ for SGD, $1.88$ for Langevin dynamics, $1.64$ for Adam, and $1.44$ for Simulated
Annealing (Figure~\ref{fig:transformer}). Two explanations are plausible, and we tested
neither: the training set is fixed at $2{,}247$ examples, which a larger model may simply fit more
easily; and the model is a pre-normalized residual architecture, a design intended to keep added
depth from degrading optimization. Either way, at this target the landscape is benign for every
method tested, and what the benchmark measures is speed and dimensional scaling, not the escape
from poor basins that the theory addresses.

\paragraph{Speed and cost.} Measured in steps, Softmin's entry time is the least sensitive to depth of the five methods: its
median stays between $340$ and $410$ steps at every depth, a spread of $1.21\times$ against
$1.49\times$ to $2.49\times$ for the others. Its position relative to the baselines follows from
that. It is faster than Simulated Annealing at every depth, by a factor of $3.8$ to $4.9$ in
median entry time, and faster than Langevin dynamics, by $1.4$ to $2.3$. Against SGD its margin
is $2.1$ at depth 2 and narrows as SGD speeds up with depth, to $1.13$ at depth 20, until SGD is
marginally ahead at depth 50 (median $328$ steps against $342$; on the mean, which the slow run
described below inflates, $327$ against $423$). Adam is the fastest method at every depth, by a
factor of $2.8$ at depth 2 and $4.0$ to $4.5$ from depth 5 onward. Wall-clock time is less
favorable to Softmin than step counts. The model evaluations are identical in number across
methods, but Softmin's ensemble update is the most expensive per step, at $2.5$\,s against
$2.0$\,s for Adam and $1.8$\,s for SGD at depth 50. SGD is therefore faster than Softmin in
wall-clock from depth 5 onward, and Softmin's wall-clock margin over Langevin dynamics vanishes at
depth 50, though it stays $2.7$ to $4.1$ times faster than Simulated Annealing.

\paragraph{Slow runs.} One Softmin run is an outlier. At depth 50, seed 2 reaches the target at step $743$, against
$333$ to $363$ for the other four seeds, while no other method is unusual on that seed. To tell a
rare tail from a fluke, we reran Softmin alone for 25 seeds at depths 20 and 50. The ten seeds
shared with the sweep reproduce its entry times and final losses exactly, so the slow run is a
property of its seed and not of hardware nondeterminism. The slow runs recur: at depth 50, five
of the 25 seeds take between $527$ and $743$ steps, separated by a clear gap from the other twenty,
which all finish within $403$; at depth 20, one seed of 25 takes $551$ steps, against at most
$385$ for the rest. The two rates do not differ significantly (Fisher's exact test, $p=0.19$).
Nothing in the loss by step $250$ singles these seeds out: at depth 50 they rank 5th, 6th, 19th,
21st and 24th of 25, and our checkpoints are too sparse to tell whether they plateau or descend
more slowly. None comes close to failing, since the slowest uses $743$ of the $2{,}000$ allowed
steps, and the medians reported above are robust to them: over 25 seeds Softmin's median is $345$
steps at depth 50 and $360$ at depth 20. We ran the additional seeds for Softmin only, so we cannot
say whether the other methods have a comparable tail: at a rate of one run in five, five seeds
would show none of it a third of the time.

\subsubsection{Ablation Study: Ascent-Descent Mechanism}
\label{apx:ablation-study}

We isolate the contribution of the sign-changing pre-factor $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$ itself by comparing the real (\emph{signed}) mechanism against two ablations: \emph{positive part}, $(\prefactor{k}{\xx})_+$, which freezes any particle the real mechanism would have pushed uphill instead of letting it ascend; and \emph{absolute value}, $|\prefactor{k}{\xx}|$, which forces every particle to always descend, at whatever magnitude the real mechanism would have applied. All three variants share the same Gibbs/softmax weighting $n\cdot w_k$ that scales $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$ in the first place -- only the sign treatment on top of it differs.

The result is not the clean "signed wins, the ablations degrade" story the sign-flipping mechanism might suggest. On the two swept well benchmarks (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation-wells}), there is no consistent winner: at the easier settings signed is competitive or best (e.g. quadruple well $A=15$: signed $0.25$, positive part $0.29$, absolute $0.34$), but at harder settings absolute is clearly faster -- double well $\delta=15$ ($h=56.1$): signed $3.81$, positive part $3.06$, absolute $2.20$; quadruple well $A=30$: signed $32.69$, positive part $30.70$, absolute $10.82$, roughly $3\times$ faster than either alternative that still preserves or freezes the sign. At the two fixed, hard settings (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation-fixed}) the three modes are close to indistinguishable: Ackley $d=50$ entry time (mean $100.1$/$98.2$/$99.4$ for signed/positive part/absolute, all 100\% reached) and Lennard-Jones $N=38$ quenched-energy gap ($2.26$/$2.48$/$2.40$) show heavily overlapping distributions.

Taken together, these four benchmarks suggest that preserving the sign of $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$ -- letting trailing particles genuinely ascend rather than merely stall or be redirected downhill -- is not what is doing most of the practical work in this noise regime ($\sigma=1$). The Gibbs/softmax weighting $n\cdot w_k$, identical across all three variants, already strongly suppresses trailing particles' contribution regardless of the sign convention applied on top of it; that shared attenuation is the more plausible source of the empirical advantage over the baselines on the well and Lennard-Jones benchmarks of Section~\ref{sec:numerical-experiments}. The sign-flipping ascent mechanism remains what Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} is stated and proved for, and is a $\sigma\to 0$ asymptotic statement -- these benchmarks run at a fixed, non-vanishing $\sigma$, and the ablation results here are consistent with the sign convention mattering more as $\sigma$ shrinks than it does at the noise level tested.

    \centering

    \caption{Ascent-descent mechanism ablation on the 2D quadruple well at a lower inverse temperature ($\beta=2$, vs.\ $\beta=10$ above; 50 seeds per point, $A=2,\dots,20$), where the Gibbs weight $n\cdot w_k$ no longer concentrates sharply enough to mask the sign convention's effect.}
    \label{fig:ablation-quadruple-well-beta2}
\end{figure}

The near-parity at $\beta=10$ raises a natural question: is it specific to that aggressive a weighting? A sharply concentrated Gibbs weight already suppresses trailing particles' contribution regardless of the sign convention applied on top of it, by the argument above, so a less concentrated weighting should let the sign convention's own effect show through more directly. Repeating the quadruple-well ablation at $\beta=2$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation-quadruple-well-beta2}) is consistent with this: at the easier settings absolute and positive part are faster than signed, sometimes markedly so (depth gap $7$: signed $2.58$, positive part $0.89$, absolute $0.88$), but this reverses once the barrier grows past a depth gap of about $10$ -- from depth gap $13$ through $19$ signed is consistently and substantially faster (e.g.\ depth gap $15$: signed $0.34$, positive part $1.89$, absolute $2.59$; depth gap $19$: signed $0.52$, positive part $1.13$, absolute $3.60$, nearly $7\times$ faster than absolute). At this lower temperature, preserving the sign of $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$ -- letting trailing particles genuinely ascend rather than merely stall or be redirected downhill -- appears to become the dominant factor as the barrier increases: in this sweep, as the escape gets harder, the variant that lets trailing particles actively climb out is the one that keeps up, which is consistent with the sign-flipping mechanism Theorem~\ref{thm:transition-time} is stated and proved for.
