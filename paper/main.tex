\begin{abstract}
    Non-convex optimization in high dimensions requires balancing gradient information with global exploration, a tension that Langevin dynamics and other gradient-informed methods address only partially. We introduce a gradient-based swarm method driven by a Softmin Energy interaction $J_\beta(\xx)$, a smooth approximation of the per-particle minimum that couples particles through their relative energies. Combining the resulting gradient flow with Brownian noise, we obtain a dynamics that retains the efficiency of gradient steps while inheriting the global-search behavior of swarm methods. We show that this interaction always partitions the ensemble into particles descending toward local minima and particles automatically repelled toward maxima, and that the same interaction caps the Freidlin--Wentzell quasipotential barrier separating adjacent basins at a level of order $1/\beta$, independently of the true barrier height, strictly below the barrier faced by an overdamped Langevin particle in the same landscape. Numerical experiments on double-well, quadruple-well, and Lennard-Jones cluster benchmarks confirm the analysis and show improved convergence over Simulated Annealing, Langevin dynamics, and variants of Particle Swarm Optimization and Consensus Based Optimization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Non-convex optimization problems are notoriously difficult, as the presence of multiple local minima can trap gradient-based algorithms. Existing approaches such as gradient descent perform well for convex functions with a single minimum but will likely converge to suboptimal local minima in more complex landscapes. This challenge is a central motivation in machine learning more broadly -- non-convex losses in deep neural network training, and sharp local minima in reinforcement learning objectives, are two prominent settings where escaping suboptimal basins is a recognized bottleneck -- though the benchmarks in this paper (Section \ref{sec:numerical-experiments}) target structured, analytically tractable landscapes rather than these settings directly, so the theoretical and empirical claims below should be read as addressing the general non-convex escape problem, not as a validation on neural-network or RL training itself.

A classical strategy to address this issue is to add a noise term to the dynamics, allowing the system to escape local minima and leading to stochastic gradient-based methods, \textcolor{purple}{such as Langevin dynamics \cite{CoffeyKalmykovWaldron2004} and noise-perturbed stochastic gradient descent \cite{fehrman2020convergence}}. \textcolor{purple}{These methods remain effective but typically exhibit slow transition times between local minima, since the mean escape time of a particle subject to Gaussian noise grows exponentially in the height of the surrounding potential barrier \cite{berglund2011kramers}.} \textcolor{purple}{Methods that improve on this convergence rate often rely on additional structural assumptions on the objective $f$, for instance a decomposition into simpler component functions amenable to specialized solvers.} In more general settings where such structure is not available, Simulated Annealing and its variants \cite{bolte2024swarm,xu2018global} are widely used, leveraging a decaying temperature schedule to shift the dynamics from noisy exploration to refined local search.

A natural extension of stochastic gradient-based approaches is the use of multiple particles to explore the search space in parallel. For instance, when particles evolve independently under Langevin dynamics, the expected transition times between local minima decrease compared to single-particle dynamics purely due to statistical averaging over multiple independent trajectories. \textcolor{purple}{Moreover, allowing particles to cooperate rather than evolve independently, as in Interacting Particle Langevin Dynamics \cite{akyildiz2025interacting,encinar2025proximal}, can further improve exploration and convergence.} The idea of particle cooperation is often central in Metaheuristic methods \cite{bandaru2016metaheuristic,yang2020nature}. Such methods provide a broad framework for global optimization and have demonstrated strong empirical performance, \textcolor{purple}{though they largely lack the rigorous theoretical convergence guarantees available for gradient-based methods \cite{hussain2019metaheuristic,rajwar2023exhaustive}.} Many such methods employ a particle-swarm-like paradigm \cite{hussain2019metaheuristic,rajwar2023exhaustive}, in which multiple interacting particles explore the search space in parallel to locate global minimizers.

The global optimization method proposed in this paper builds on these ideas by combining stochastic gradient techniques with cooperative multi-particle dynamics. We employ the well-known Softmin Energy functional $J_\beta$, which smoothly interpolates between the average and the hard minimum of the function values across particle positions. This functional acts as the source of interaction among particles, whose evolution is governed by its gradient, driving the system toward more effective exploration and optimization.

The Softmin function provides a natural way to interpolate between uniform weighting and hard minima via temperature $\beta>0$. A common use of the Softmin function is to define the Gibbs distribution, where the probability of sampling index $i$ out of values $v_1,\dots,v_n$ is given by:
\[
    \mathbb{P}[i] = \tfrac{\exp{(-\beta{v_i})}}{\sum_{j=1}^n \exp{(-\beta v_j})},
\]
with $\beta\to\infty$ concentrating on $\min_{i} v_i$ and $\beta\to0$ uniform \cite{bertsekas1997nonlinear}. This underlies stochastic optimization \cite{ackley1985boltzmann, srivastava2012multimodal, gelfand1990sampling,gelfand2000gibbs} and exploration in Reinforcement Learning \cite{pan2019reinforcement, haarnoja2017reinforcement}.

Our proposed method is a stochastic gradient-based particle swarm approach that leverages a Softmin-type energy functional to effectively incorporate gradient information from the particles. Consider a differentiable function $f:\R^d\to\R$. The objective is to approximately solve the global minimization problem $\min_{x\in\R^d}f(x)$. Given a population of $n$ particles $\xx=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ with $x_i\in\R^d$ for all $i\in[n]$, the Softmin Energy is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    J_\beta(\xx)=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\exp(-\beta f(x_i))}{\sum_{j=1}^n\exp(-\beta f(x_j))}f(x_i),
    \label{eq:Softmin-energy}
\end{equation}
which provides a smooth approximation of the empirical minimum among the $f(x_i)$. The parameter $\beta>0$ controls the approximation's sharpness: larger values yield a closer approximation to the hard minimum $\min_i f(x_i)$, while $\beta\to0$ recovers the average $\frac 1 n \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i)$.

To minimize $J_\beta$ we consider the stochastic gradient flow in the particle space $\R^{nd}$:
\begin{equation}\label{e:sde}
   \dd\xx_t = -n\nabla J_{\beta_t}(\xx_t)\dd t+\sqrt{2\sigma}\dd B_t,
\end{equation}
where $(B_t)_{t\geq 0}$ represents a standard $nd$-dimensional Brownian motion and $\sigma>0$ introduces stochasticity to promote exploration and escape from local minima. \textcolor{purple}{The inverse temperature $\beta_t$ may vary over time: the ensemble-descent guarantee of Theorem \ref{thm:ensemble-min} holds for an arbitrary such schedule, but the barrier-flattening analysis of \S\ref{sec:hitting-times} fixes $\beta$ to a constant, and Remark \ref{rem:higher-dim} reports that, empirically, a fixed, well-tuned $\beta$ outperforms annealing it.}

\subsection{Contribution}
The main novelty of this work is the rigorous analysis of stochastic gradient-based dynamics driven by the Softmin Energy functional, $J_\beta(\xx)$. In this paper, we prove that the resulting inter-particle interaction mechanism provides \textcolor{purple}{two concrete theoretical guarantees, detailed below, that are absent from standard gradient-based and non-interacting stochastic methods for non-convex global optimization: a no-slowdown guarantee on the ensemble's best objective value, and a provable cap on the effective escape barrier between basins.}

\paragraph{Dynamic Ensemble Partitioning and Accelerated Convergence} The interaction induced by $\nabla J_\beta$ naturally partitions the particle ensemble via an effective learning rate, $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$. At every stable equilibrium, and without any convexity assumption on $f$, particles below the Softmin threshold ($\prefactor{k}{\xx} > 0$) sit at local minima, while trapped, high-energy particles ($\prefactor{k}{\xx} < 0$) are automatically repelled to hill-climb and explore, with at least one particle always at a local minimum (Theorem \ref{thm:stationary-points}). \textcolor{purple}{This sign is not fixed to any single particle over time, so no individual trajectory is permanently assigned the minimizing role; nonetheless, the ensemble's running best objective value is always nonincreasing, and, whenever $f$ satisfies a Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition on the initial sublevel set, it converges to the global minimum at least as fast as ordinary gradient descent, for any inverse-temperature schedule (Theorem \ref{thm:ensemble-min}).}

\paragraph{Provable Barrier Flattening} We demonstrate that the Softmin dynamics do not merely reduce effective potential barriers, but analytically cap them. By integrating the effective drift along an explicit escape channel, we prove that arbitrarily high physical barriers separating adjacent basins are capped, at the level of the Freidlin--Wentzell quasipotential, to a constant of order $1/\beta$, uniformly in the true barrier height (Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time}). This bound holds unconditionally on the quasipotential itself, strictly below the exact small-noise exit exponent of an overdamped Langevin particle in the same landscape; turning it into an exponentially cheaper guarantee on the expected transition time additionally requires the metastable-domain hypotheses made precise in Corollary \ref{cor:exit-time-conditional}. This structural landscape modulation is a direct consequence of the continuous Softmin repulsion, distinguishing our approach from generic stochastic swarm methods and Simulated Annealing.

\subsection{Related work}

\paragraph{Metaheuristic optimization methods} 
Metaheuristic optimization methods include Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO) \cite{CarrilloChoiTotzeckTse2018,fornasier2024consensus,pinnau2017consensus, carrillo2021consensus}, Genetic Algorithms \cite{Goldberg1989}, Ant Colony Optimization \cite{DorigoStutzle2004}, and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) \cite{kennedy1995particle, noel2012new}. Traditional particle-swarm approaches typically operate without explicit gradient information, relying instead on position-based updates to coordinate particles toward promising regions. When reliable gradient information is available, leveraging it enables convergence rates comparable to standard stochastic gradient descent, which gradient-agnostic metaheuristics cannot achieve.

Recent gradient-based swarm methods \cite{riedl2023gradient, bolte2024swarm, tang2024discrete, carrillo2021consensus,noel2012new}, have made progress by incorporating gradient information into multi-particle dynamics. However, our approach differs fundamentally in its mechanism for inducing exploration and escaping barriers. Rather than consensus-based averaging or general gradient-weighted potentials, we employ the Softmin Energy functional to explicitly create a minimizing/maximizing partition of the particle ensemble: particles below the Softmin threshold are drawn toward local minima via gradient descent, while particles above the threshold are repelled toward maxima. This automatic role assignment (Theorem \ref{thm:stationary-points}) simultaneously reduces effective potential barriers (Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time}) and forces suboptimal particles into exploratory regimes without requiring manual basin-switching heuristics.

To the best of our knowledge, no other metaheuristic employs the Softmin Energy functional, computed as the softmin of function values at particle positions, as the sole source of inter-particle interaction. This design enables provable escape from suboptimal local minima with a barrier bounded independently of the true barrier height (Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time}, Section \ref{sec:hitting-times}), together with a deterministic guarantee that the ensemble's best value never lags ordinary gradient flow under a Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition (Theorem \ref{thm:ensemble-min}, Section \ref{sec:convergence-rate}), as validated experimentally on benchmark landscapes where barriers dominate the hardness.

\paragraph{Langevin-based sampling and interacting-particle methods}
Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD) \cite{welling2011bayesian} and its non-asymptotic analysis for non-convex optimization \cite{raginsky2017nonconvex} study a single diffusion whose drift is the gradient of the objective itself, with guarantees governed by the spectral gap of the associated Gibbs measure; when multiple particles are used, interaction typically enters only through averaging over independent runs. Interacting Particle Langevin Dynamics (IPLD) \cite{akyildiz2025interacting, encinar2025proximal} instead couples an ensemble of particles through their empirical measure, using this mean-field interaction to build unbiased or stabilized estimators of the marginal likelihood gradient in latent-variable models. Our construction is closer in spirit to IPLD, in that it also relies on genuine ensemble-level coupling rather than independent copies, but the interaction itself is different in kind: instead of pooling particles through an empirical average, the Softmin Energy $J_\beta$ couples particles through their relative energies, producing a state-dependent, sign-changing effective learning rate $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$ that deterministically assigns each particle a minimizing or maximizing role (Theorem \ref{thm:stationary-points}). It is this competitive, temperature-sharpened coupling, rather than mean-field averaging, that produces the barrier-capping mechanism of Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time}, and it is aimed directly at global optimization of $f$ rather than at marginal-likelihood estimation.

\paragraph{Connection to Statistical Physics and Functional Design}
Our approach shares conceptual roots with population-based statistical physics methods such as Population Annealing (PA) \cite{Wang_2015}, which also employs a Gibbs-weighted ensemble to locate global ground states. However, while PA relies on discrete demographic selection, duplicating low-energy particles and eliminating high-energy ones prior to local Markov Chain Monte Carlo steps, our method maintains a fixed population size and drives exploration through continuous dynamical repulsion. This active exploration mechanism differentiates our Softmin Energy functional from the standard log-sum-exp (or free-energy) functional, $F_\beta(\xx) = -{\beta}^{-1} \log \sum e^{-\beta f(x_i)}$. The gradient of the log-sum-exp formulation, $\nabla_{x_k} F_\beta(\xx) = w_k \nabla f(x_k)$, strictly preserves gradient descent, causing sub-optimal particles with small weights to simply stagnate. In contrast, the gradient of our proposed Softmin functional, $J_\beta(\xx) = \sum w_i f(x_i)$, natively incorporates the sign-changing effective learning rate, $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$. Consequently, rather than discarding trapped particles, the geometry of the Softmin functional actively destabilizes their local minima, forcing high-energy particles to hill-climb and escape via the energy barrier-flattening mechanism formalized in our theoretical analysis.

\paragraph{Softmin Energy} Theoretical work on the Gibbs Softmin (or Boltzmann Softmin) has primarily focused on its properties as a smooth approximation to the minimum operator \cite{bertsekas1997nonlinear}, convergence of softmax policy gradients in reinforcement learning \cite{mei2020global}, and its role in exploration–exploitation trade-offs via temperature scheduling \cite{song2019revisiting}. To our knowledge, this is the first work employing $J_\beta$ as an optimization objective for non-convex global search. \textcolor{purple}{This is conceptually distinct from swarm-gradient methods that anneal a mean-field particle density toward the Dirac mass at the global minimizer \cite{bolte2024swarm}: rather than acting on a population density, the Softmin Energy couples particles directly through the relative ranking of their objective values.} The effective barrier reduction via particle cooperation, analyzed in Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time}, is previously unstudied.

In Section \ref{sec:gradient-flow-dynamics}, we analyze the deterministic gradient flow dynamics obtained by setting $\sigma =0$ in \eqref{e:sde} in detail. Section \ref{sec:stationary-points} characterizes the stable equilibria of this flow with no convexity assumption on $f$: every particle sits at a critical point of $f$, the sign of its effective learning rate fixes whether that critical point is a minimum or a maximum, and at least one particle always resides at a local minimum. \section{Gradient Flow dynamics for Softmin Energy}
\label{sec:gradient-flow-dynamics}

This section focuses on the deterministic gradient flow dynamics
\begin{equation}
    \dd\xx_t = -n\nabla J_\beta(\xx_t)\dd t,
    \label{eq:gradient-flow}
\end{equation}
where $\xx_t = (x_1(t), \dots, x_n(t))$ denotes the state of the particle system and the Softmin Energy $J_\beta(\xx)$ is defined in \eqref{eq:Softmin-energy}. The scaling factor $n$ is fixed by the requirement that the dynamics reduce, in the high-temperature limit $\beta\to0$, to $n$ independent gradient particles: as shown below, every Gibbs weight $w_k\to1/n$ and every \textcolor{purple}{pre-factor $\prefactor{k}{\xx}\to1$ (defined precisely in \eqref{eq:softmin-gradient} below)} in this limit, so \eqref{eq:gradient-flow} recovers $\dot x_k=-\nabla f(x_k)$ componentwise. It also ensures that the subsequent derivatives and governing equations remain well-posed in the mean-field limit $n \to \infty$. This continuous-time system performs gradient descent on the Softmin landscape, which is formulated such that its minimization inherently drives the ensemble toward the minima of the objective function $f$.

Subsequent analysis characterizes the stable equilibria of this flow without any convexity assumption on $f$: at every such equilibrium, each particle sits at a critical point of $f$, the sign of its effective learning rate $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}$ fixes whether that critical point is a minimum or a maximum, and at least one particle resides at a local minimum. Separately, we show that \textcolor{purple}{the ensemble's running best objective value is always nonincreasing, and decreases at least as fast as ordinary gradient descent toward the global minimum whenever $f$ satisfies a Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition}, for any inverse temperature schedule.

To formally analyze these dynamics, it is necessary to first evaluate the gradient of $nJ_\beta$ with respect to the individual particle coordinates. Let $w_k = e^{-\beta f(x_k)}(\sum_j e^{-\beta f(x_j)})^{-1}$ denote the Gibbs weight of particle $x_k$, and define the dynamic pre-factor $\prefactor{k}{\xx} = nw_k\left[1 - \beta(f(x_k) - J_\beta(\xx))\right]$. The gradient of $nJ_\beta(\xx)$ with respect to particle $x_k$ is computed in Lemma \ref{lem:derivative-of-J-proof} and is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{x_k} (nJ_\beta(\xx)) =\prefactor{k}{\xx} \nabla f(x_k),
    \label{eq:softmin-gradient}
\end{equation}
so that \eqref{eq:gradient-flow} reads componentwise as $\dot x_k=-\prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla f(x_k)$. The exact second-order partial derivatives and corresponding Hessian blocks, which are required for the subsequent local stability analysis, are deferred to Appendix \ref{lem:derivative-of-J-proof}.

This formulation reveals a critical property of the Softmin gradient. The dynamic pre-factor $\prefactor{k}{\xx} = nw_k\beta\left[1/\beta - f(x_k) + J_\beta(\xx)\right]$ dictates the effective direction of the gradient flow for each particle $x_k$, acting as a state-dependent learning rate. According to the dynamics in \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}, if a particle evaluates to a landscape value $f(x_k) < J_\beta(\xx) + 1/\beta$, the pre-factor $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$ is strictly positive, and the particle performs standard gradient descent on $f$; the particle is then said to be \textit{minimizing}. Conversely, if $f(x_k) > J_\beta(\xx) + 1/\beta$, the pre-factor becomes negative, effectively reversing the flow, and the particle is \textit{maximizing}, executing gradient ascent on $f$. This mechanism theoretically guarantees that particles trapped in sub-optimal local minima are actively repelled and forced to explore the landscape, whereas particles at comparatively lower energy states continue to minimize $f$. \textcolor{purple}{Throughout, we refer to $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$ as the pre-factor, and use the term \emph{effective learning rate} specifically when emphasizing this interpretation of it as a state-dependent scaling of the gradient step.}

Two elementary facts drive the equilibrium analysis that follows. First, summing the pre-factor over all particles and using $\sum_k w_k=1$ and $\sum_k w_k f(x_k)=J_\beta(\xx)$,
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{k=1}^n \prefactor{k}{\xx} = n \qquad \text{for every } \xx\in\R^{nd},
    \label{eq:sum-prefactor}
\end{equation}
so at least one coefficient is always strictly positive. Second, at any configuration where every particle sits at a critical point of $f$, the mixed second partial derivatives of $J_\beta$ vanish identically, since each mixed Hessian block is an outer product carrying a factor $\nabla f(x_k)$ (Lemma \ref{lem:derivative-of-J-proof}); the Hessian of $nJ_\beta$ is then block diagonal.

\subsection{Stationary points of the dynamics}
\label{sec:stationary-points}

This subsection characterizes the stable equilibria of the gradient flow \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}. Theorem \ref{thm:stationary-points} shows that at any such equilibrium every particle sits at a critical point of $f$ with a strictly nonzero pre-factor, the sign of that pre-factor fixes whether the critical point is a minimum or a maximum, and at least one particle always resides at a local minimum. Consequently, the Softmin formulation guarantees both the exploitation of deep minima and the systematic destabilization of higher-energy critical points, without requiring any local geometric hypothesis on $f$.

\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:stationary-points}
    Let $f\in C^2(\R^d)$, let $\beta>0$, and let $\bar\xx=(\bar x_1,\dots,\bar x_n)$ be a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium of \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}. Then, for every $k\in\{1,\dots,n\}$, evaluated at $\bar\xx$:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\nabla f(\bar x_k)=0$;
        \item $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}\ne0$;
        \item the sign of $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}$ fixes the type of $\bar x_k$:
        \[
            \prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}>0 \implies \bar x_k \text{ is a local minimum of } f,
            \qquad
            \prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}<0 \implies \bar x_k \text{ is a local maximum of } f,
        \]
        with $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}>0 \iff f(\bar x_k)<J_\beta(\bar\xx)+1/\beta$.
    \end{enumerate}
    Moreover, by \eqref{eq:sum-prefactor}, at least one particle sits at a local minimum of $f$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    The rigorous derivation of these stability conditions is deferred to Appendix \ref{apx:technical-proofs}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Sufficient condition for stability]
\label{cor:stability-sufficient}
Let $\bar\xx=(\bar x_1,\dots,\bar x_n)$ satisfy $\nabla f(\bar x_k)=0$ for every $k$, and assume that for each $k$ either
\[
    \prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}>0 \text{ and } \nabla^2f(\bar x_k)\succ0,
    \qquad\text{or}\qquad
    \prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}<0 \text{ and } \nabla^2f(\bar x_k)\prec0,
\]
evaluated at $\bar\xx$. Then $\nabla^2(nJ_\beta)(\bar\xx)\succ0$; in particular $\bar\xx$ is a strict local minimum of $nJ_\beta$ and a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium of \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    By Lemma \ref{lem:derivative-of-J-proof} the Hessian at a fully critical configuration is block diagonal with blocks $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}\nabla^2f(\bar x_k)$, and each hypothesis makes the corresponding block positive definite.
\end{proof}

\textcolor{purple}{By Theorem \ref{thm:stationary-points} and Corollary \ref{cor:stability-sufficient}, the deterministic flow admits stable equilibria at which particles sit at local minima and local maxima of $f$ simultaneously, not only at full consensus on a single minimum; which equilibrium is reached, and hence whether it is globally optimal, depends on the initialization, and the deterministic dynamics offer no guarantee of escaping a suboptimal one. This motivates injecting noise:} the noisy dynamics analyzed in Section \ref{sec:hitting-times} provide a mechanism for the ensemble to escape such traps.

\subsection{Descent of the ensemble minimum}
\label{sec:convergence-rate}

\textcolor{purple}{The instantaneous sign of the pre-factor $\prefactor{k}{\xx(t)}$ is not conserved for a fixed particle, since it depends on every coordinate through $J_\beta$; we therefore track instead the running best value across the ensemble,
\[
    m(t):=\min_{1\le k\le n}f(x_k(t)),
\]
controlling it for a general, possibly time-dependent, inverse-temperature schedule $\beta_t>0$, where $\prefactor{k}{\xx(t)}$ denotes the pre-factor evaluated at inverse temperature $\beta_t$ and configuration $\xx(t)$.}

\begin{definition}[Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition]
    \label{def:pl-condition}
    A differentiable function $f:\R^d\to\R$ satisfies the \textit{Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz (PL) condition} on a set $S\subseteq\R^d$ with constant $\mu>0$ if
    \[
        \tfrac12\|\nabla f(x)\|^2\ge\mu\bigl(f(x)-f_\star\bigr), \qquad f_\star:=\inf f,
    \]
    for every $x\in S$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Descent of the ensemble minimum]
    \label{thm:ensemble-min}
    Let $f\in C^1(\R^d)$ have locally Lipschitz gradient, let $\beta_t>0$ be measurable, and let $\xx(t)=(x_1(t),\dots,x_n(t))$ be a global solution of
    \[
        \dot x_k(t)=-\prefactor{k}{\xx(t)}\,\nabla f(x_k(t)), \qquad k=1,\dots,n.
    \]
    Suppose $f$ satisfies the Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition (Definition \ref{def:pl-condition}) with constant $\mu>0$ on the sublevel set $S_0:=\{x:f(x)\le m(0)\}$. Then $m$ is nonincreasing and
    \[
        m(t)-f_\star\le e^{-2\mu t}\bigl(m(0)-f_\star\bigr), \qquad t\ge0.
    \]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    The proof is deferred to Appendix \ref{apx:technical-proofs}.
\end{proof}

The estimate compares the best value in the ensemble against ordinary gradient flow. Note that the Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz hypothesis is imposed on the sublevel set $S_0$ itself: it holds automatically whenever $S_0$ contains no suboptimal critical point of $f$, and Theorem \ref{thm:ensemble-min} should be read as a no-slowdown guarantee for the ensemble's leading particle in that regime, complementary to the basin-escape mechanism of Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time}, rather than as a global convergence-rate result. \textcolor{purple}{Without the Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz hypothesis, $m$ remains nonincreasing by the same argument (Appendix \ref{apx:technical-proofs}); it is only the exponential rate of convergence specifically to the global value $f_\star$ that requires it.}

\begin{remark}[Global existence]
    \label{rem:global-existence}
    Theorem \ref{thm:ensemble-min} presupposes a global solution. Since $m$ is nonincreasing, every particle attaining the running minimum stays in the compact sublevel set $S_0$ when $f$ is coercive; the theorem controls these particles only. Maximizing particles ($\prefactor{k}{\xx(t)}<0$) are damped by an exponentially small Gibbs weight, but their trajectories are not controlled by this estimate, and global existence must be assumed or established separately.
\end{remark}

\section{Barrier Flattening and Exit Asymptotics}
\label{sec:hitting-times}

\textcolor{purple}{This section fixes the inverse temperature in \eqref{e:sde}, $\beta_t\equiv\beta$}, with the noise term $\sqrt{2\sigma}\dd B_t$ introducing isotropic $nd$-dimensional Brownian noise. Componentwise,
\begin{equation}
    \dd x_k(t) = -\prefactor{k}{\xx_t}\nabla f(x_k(t))\dd t + \sqrt{2\sigma}\dd B_t^k,
    \label{eq:softmin-langevin}
\end{equation}
so $\prefactor{k}{\xx_t}$ acts as a state-dependent, sign-changing effective learning rate for particle $x_k$, as established in Section \ref{sec:gradient-flow-dynamics}. The rigorous exit analysis below is carried out for physical dimension $d=1$\textcolor{purple}{; the extension to $d>1$ is discussed in Remark \ref{rem:higher-dim}}.

\subsection{One-dimensional basin exit and post-saddle passage}
\label{sec:exit-times}

Fix $n\ge2$, $\beta>0$, and $d=1$. Consider three consecutive nondegenerate critical points of $f$,
\[
    z_-<s<z_+,
\]
with $z_\pm$ local minima and $s$ the separating local maximum, and assume
\begin{equation}
    f'(x)>0 \text{ on } (z_-,s), \qquad f'(x)<0 \text{ on } (s,z_+).
    \label{eq:adjacent-well-monotonicity}
\end{equation}
Set the relative energy and the barrier height
\begin{equation}
    r(x):=f(x)-f(z_-), \qquad h:=r(s)>0, \qquad \rho_+:=r(z_+)<h.
    \label{eq:relative-energy}
\end{equation}
The case of interest is $\rho_+<0$, in which $z_+$ is a strictly deeper minimum than $z_-$.

\begin{assumption}
\label{assum:channel}
$f\in C^2(\R)$; $z_-<s<z_+$ are nondegenerate critical points of $f$ (i.e.\ $f''(z_-)>0$, $f''(s)<0$, $f''(z_+)>0$) satisfying the monotonicity condition \eqref{eq:adjacent-well-monotonicity}; and $n\ge2$, $\beta>0$, $d=1$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{remark}[Metastability of the initial state]
\label{rem:metastable-init}
At the consensus configuration $\bar\xx=(z_-,\dots,z_-)$, every Gibbs weight equals $1/n$, so $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}\equiv1$ for every $k$. By Corollary \ref{cor:stability-sufficient}, using $f''(z_-)>0$, $\bar\xx$ is therefore a strict local minimum of $nJ_\beta$ and a locally asymptotically stable equilibrium of \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}, i.e.\ genuinely metastable for the noisy dynamics \eqref{e:sde} at small $\sigma$.
\end{remark}

We rely on the Freidlin--Wentzell large deviation theory for exit times of small-noise diffusions from metastable domains, summarized in Appendix \ref{apx:fw-theory}: under the standard hypotheses of that theory, the exit time $\tau_D^\sigma$ from a metastable domain $D$ satisfies $\lim_{\sigma\downarrow0}\sigma\log \E_{\xx}\tau_D^\sigma=V_\beta(\xx,\partial D)$, where $V_\beta$ is the Freidlin--Wentzell quasipotential associated with \eqref{e:sde} at fixed $\beta$.

To build an admissible escape path, freeze $n-1$ particles at $z_-$ and let one particle move:
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma(x):=(x,z_-,\dots,z_-).
    \label{eq:channel}
\end{equation}
Factoring $e^{-\beta f(z_-)}$ out of the Gibbs weights gives $J_\beta(\Gamma(x))=f(z_-)+u(r(x))/n$, that is, the exact identity
\begin{equation}
    nJ_\beta(\Gamma(x))=nf(z_-)+u(r(x)), \qquad u(r):=\frac{n\,r\,e^{-\beta r}}{\,n-1+e^{-\beta r}\,}, \quad r\in\R,
    \label{eq:reduced-potential}
\end{equation}
where $u$ is the \emph{reduced potential}. The additive constant $nf(z_-)$ does not enter $\nabla(nJ_\beta)$, and both the action and the quasipotential depend on $nJ_\beta$ only through its increments.

\begin{lemma}[Channel invariance and reduced gradient flow]
\label{lem:channel-invariant}
The set $\{\Gamma(x):x\in\R\}$ is invariant under \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}, since $f'(z_-)=0$ forces the frozen coordinates' drift to vanish identically. On this invariant manifold, the dynamics reduces to the one-dimensional gradient flow $\dot x=-W'(x)$ of the reduced potential $W(x):=u(r(x))$ of \eqref{eq:reduced-potential}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $z_-$ is a critical point of $f$, every frozen coordinate satisfies $\dot x_j=-\prefactor{j}{\Gamma(x)}f'(z_-)=0$ by \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}, so $\{\Gamma(x):x\in\R\}$ is invariant. Differentiating \eqref{eq:reduced-potential} along the channel gives $W'(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[nJ_\beta(\Gamma(x))\bigr]=u'(r(x))r'(x)=u'(r(x))f'(x)$; applying the chain rule directly to $nJ_\beta$ and \eqref{eq:softmin-gradient} gives the same derivative as $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}f'(x)$. Hence $W'(x)=\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}f'(x)$, matching the active coordinate's drift $-\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}f'(x)$ in \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}, and in particular reproving $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}=u'(r(x))$, made explicit in closed form in Lemma \ref{lem:channel-drift} below.
\end{proof}

The reduced potential also records the sign of the drift of the active particle, and this is what fixes the geometry of the channel.

\begin{lemma}[Channel drift and shape of the reduced potential]
\label{lem:channel-drift}
Let $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}$ denote the pre-factor of the active particle along the channel. Then, for every $x\in\R$,
\begin{equation}
    \prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}=u'(r(x))
    =\frac{n\,e^{-\beta r(x)}\,q(r(x))}{\bigl(n-1+e^{-\beta r(x)}\bigr)^{2}},
    \qquad
    q(r):=(n-1)(1-\beta r)+e^{-\beta r}.
    \label{eq:a1-equals-uprime}
\end{equation}
The function $q$ is strictly decreasing on $\R$ and has a unique zero $r_c$, which satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \frac1\beta<r_c<\frac{n}{(n-1)\beta}.
    \label{eq:rc-location}
\end{equation}
Consequently $u$ is strictly increasing on $(-\infty,r_c]$ and strictly decreasing on $[r_c,\infty)$, and $r_c$ is exactly the relative-energy level at which the active particle switches from minimizing to maximizing, i.e.\ the level at which $f(x)-J_\beta(\Gamma(x))=1/\beta$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    See Appendix \ref{apx:technical-proofs}.
\end{proof}

We abbreviate the two relevant channel heights by
\begin{equation}
    u_c:=u(r_c)=\max_{r\in\R}u(r),
    \qquad
    u_h:=\max_{0\le r\le h}u(r)=u\bigl(\min\{h,r_c\}\bigr).
    \label{eq:channel-heights}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[One-dimensional basin exit and post-saddle passage]
\label{thm:transition-time}
Under Assumption \ref{assum:channel}, the following hold.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{(Uniform barrier cap.)}
    \begin{equation}
        u_c=\max_{r\in\R}u(r)<\frac{n}{e(n-1)\beta}.
        \label{eq:barrier-cap}
    \end{equation}
    \textcolor{purple}{Both $u_c$ and, by \eqref{eq:rc-location}, $r_c$ are of order $\beta^{-1}$, uniformly in $h$.}

    \item \textbf{(Initial basin exit: quasipotential bound.)}
    The \textcolor{purple}{infimum of the action} among channel paths joining $\Gamma(z_-)$ to $\Gamma(s)$ equals $u_h$. Consequently
    \begin{equation}
        V_\beta\bigl(\Gamma(z_-),\Gamma(s)\bigr)\le u_h<\min\{h,r_c\}\le h.
        \label{eq:initial-bound}
    \end{equation}

    \item \textbf{(Post-saddle well and passage.)}
    Assume
    \begin{equation}
        r_c<h.
        \label{eq:high-barrier-regime}
    \end{equation}
    \textcolor{purple}{Then $\Gamma(s)$ is a strict local minimum of $nJ_\beta$, with channel barrier $u_c-u(h)$ on the $z_-$ side. On the $z_+$ side:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item If $\rho_+<r_c$, there is a unique $\xi_+\in(s,z_+)$ with $r(\xi_+)=r_c$, the channel well at $\Gamma(s)$ has matching barrier height $u_c-u(h)$ on this side, and
        \begin{equation}
            V_\beta\bigl(\Gamma(s),\Gamma(\xi_+)\bigr)\le u_c-u(h)<u_c;
            \label{eq:post-saddle-bound}
        \end{equation}
        \item If instead $\rho_+\ge r_c$, no point $\xi_+$ exists and \textcolor{purple}{the channel potential rises monotonically from $\Gamma(s)$ to $\Gamma(z_+)$, so no intermediate barrier separates them, with total rise $u(\rho_+)-u(h)\le u_c-u(h)$}.
    \end{itemize}
    When $\rho_+<r_c$, concatenating the two crossings gives
    \begin{equation}
        V_\beta\bigl(\Gamma(z_-),\Gamma(\xi_+)\bigr) \;\le\; u_h+\bigl(u_c-u(h)\bigr) \;\le\; 2u_c-u(h) \;<\; \frac{2n}{e(n-1)\beta},
        \label{eq:two-stage-bound}
    \end{equation}
    by \eqref{eq:initial-bound}, \eqref{eq:post-saddle-bound}, and $u_h\le u_c$.
    If instead $h\le r_c$, $\Gamma(s)$ is not a local minimum of $nJ_\beta$ and no post-saddle well forms.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    See Appendix \ref{apx:technical-proofs}.
\end{proof}

The scope of Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time} and its relation to the exact exit-time exponent is discussed in Remarks \ref{rem:scope}--\ref{rem:higher-dim} below.

\begin{corollary}[Exit-time consequence, conditional]
\label{cor:exit-time-conditional}
Suppose in addition that $z_-$ admits a metastable domain $D_-\subset\R^n$ for the flow \eqref{eq:gradient-flow}, in the sense that $\Gamma(z_-)$ is the unique attractor of $D_-$ for the noiseless flow and the hypotheses of the Freidlin--Wentzell exit theorem (Appendix \ref{apx:fw-theory}) hold on $D_-$, with $\Gamma(x_m)\in\partial D_-$ realizing the minimal quasipotential to $\partial D_-$, where $x_m\in(z_-,s]$ is the unique point with $r(x_m)=\min\{h,r_c\}$. Then
\[
    \lim_{\sigma\downarrow0}\sigma\log \E_{\Gamma(z_-)}\tau_{D_-}^\sigma
    =V_\beta\bigl(\Gamma(z_-),\partial D_-\bigr)\le u_h<h.
\]
\end{corollary}
We do not construct $D_-$ or verify these hypotheses here; Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time} supplies only the quasipotential bound \eqref{eq:initial-bound}, which holds unconditionally.

\begin{remark}[Low-barrier regime: acceleration without sign reversal]
\label{rem:low-barrier}
By \eqref{eq:a1-equals-uprime}, $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}>0$ precisely when $r(x)<r_c$. Hence $u_h=u(h)$, i.e.\ $\min\{h,r_c\}=h$, exactly in the regime $h\le r_c$, and there the drift of the active particle is minimizing along the entire crossing: the sign-reversal mechanism is never activated, the crossing is made entirely against the drift, and by Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time}(3) no Softmin-induced well appears at $s$. The acceleration is nonetheless strict, since $u(r)/r=ne^{-\beta r}/(n-1+e^{-\beta r})<1$ for $r>0$ gives $u(h)<h$. In this regime the gain comes purely from the Gibbs-weight damping of the leading particle's contribution to $nJ_\beta$, not from a sign reversal. By \eqref{eq:rc-location} the regime is $h\lesssim\beta^{-1}$; the sign-reversal mechanism is what handles barriers beyond that scale.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Interpretation and scope]
\label{rem:scope}
Part 1 shows that, along this channel, an arbitrarily high physical barrier $h$ is flattened to a level of order $\beta^{-1}$: as $h$ grows the active particle receives exponentially small Gibbs weight, so the barrier saturates rather than growing with $h$. Part 2 gives a strict full-system \emph{upper} bound on the exit exponent through an explicit admissible path; because $V_\beta$ is an infimum over all paths in $\R^n$, the channel construction can only over-estimate it. It is a bound on the Freidlin--Wentzell quasipotential itself, established by exhibiting an admissible path; it does not yet assert anything about $\E\tau^\sigma$, for which see Corollary \ref{cor:exit-time-conditional}. Part 3 addresses the post-saddle obstruction: a high barrier turns the physical maximum $s$ into a Softmin-induced well. When $\rho_+<r_c$ this well has equal-height barriers, with depth $u_c-u(h)$ strictly below the initial barrier $u_c$, so it is not a worse obstruction than the one already crossed, giving the composite two-stage bound \eqref{eq:two-stage-bound}; when $\rho_+\ge r_c$ \textcolor{purple}{the $z_+$ side presents no intermediate barrier at all, and its monotone rise $u(\rho_+)-u(h)$ is itself bounded by $u_c-u(h)$, so this case is only more favorable}. \textcolor{purple}{The two-stage exit-time exponent is governed by the larger of the two crossings, $\max\{u_h,u_c-u(h)\}\le u_c$, not by their sum, since the dominant crossing sets the asymptotic rate.} The theorem does \emph{not} identify the exact transition exponent between the two basins, which would require ruling out returns to the initial basin and competing collective paths in $\R^n$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Comparison with independent Langevin dynamics]
\label{rem:comparison}
For a single overdamped Langevin particle in the potential $f$ started at $z_-$, the classical small-noise exit exponent from the basin of $z_-$ is the physical barrier $h=f(s)-f(z_-)$. For $n$ \emph{independent} copies, the first of the $n$ exit times has the same exponential scale $h$: taking a minimum over $n$ i.i.d.\ variables with common exponential rate $h/\sigma$ changes only the multiplicative prefactor, by a factor $1/n$, not the exponent, as $\sigma\downarrow0$.

By \eqref{eq:initial-bound}, the channel construction gives $V_\beta(\Gamma(z_-),\Gamma(s))\le u_h<h$ for the joint Softmin diffusion, in \emph{every} regime of $h$ (see Remark \ref{rem:low-barrier}). This is a comparison of an \emph{upper bound on a quasipotential} for the interacting system against the \emph{exact} exponent $h$ of the noninteracting one; it shows the admissible channel gives a strictly lower quasipotential bound than independent escape, not that the true joint exit exponent is smaller. The stronger, exit-\emph{time} statement that the interaction cannot slow escape holds only under the hypotheses of Corollary \ref{cor:exit-time-conditional}, which are not verified in this paper.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Higher dimensions, temperature, and scale]
\label{rem:higher-dim}
The channel identity of Part 1 extends algebraically to $d>1$: at a critical point $s$ of $f$ the active Hessian block is $u'(h)\nabla^2 f(s)$, so if $s$ is an index-one saddle and $u'(h)<0$ the induced local minimum is a genuinely one-dimensional phenomenon, and transverse unstable directions may open cheaper escape routes. \textcolor{purple}{Although Theorem \ref{thm:ensemble-min} holds for an arbitrary schedule $\beta_t$, and \S\ref{sec:hitting-times} fixed $\beta$ purely for tractability of the exit-time analysis, a natural question is whether annealing $\beta$ helps in practice.} Regarding temperature, the fixed-$\beta$ bound \eqref{eq:barrier-cap} decreases like $\beta^{-1}$, favoring large $\beta$ for barrier reduction; but large $\beta$ also suppresses the drift of non-leading particles through their Gibbs weights, since a particle trailing the ensemble minimum by a gap $\Delta$ is weighted by $e^{-\beta\Delta}$. Both limits degrade the mechanism: as $\beta\to\infty$ the ensemble degenerates to leader-only drift, while as $\beta\to0$ the Softmin coupling degenerates toward independent noisy gradient descent and \eqref{eq:barrier-cap}'s bound itself diverges. A decaying (or, symmetrically, an increasing) schedule $\beta_t$ is a natural candidate for balancing these two effects over time; however, we found empirically that neither improves on a well-chosen \emph{fixed} $\beta$ on the benchmarks considered here (Section \ref{sec:numerical-experiments}). Both a geometric decay from a large starting $\beta$ and a geometric growth from a small one consistently reach the target \emph{more slowly} than a fixed $\beta$ tuned once via the diagnostic of Appendix \ref{app:hyperparameters}, with the gap widening at harder barrier heights, and an aggressive growth rate can drive $\beta$ to numerical overflow before the schedule ever reaches a useful range. We report this as a negative empirical result rather than infer a general claim about annealing from it: a rigorous large-deviation treatment of $\beta_t$, and of whether some other schedule shape or landscape can realize the exploration/refinement trade-off this remark motivates, remain open.
\end{remark}

\section{Numerical Experiments}
\label{sec:numerical-experiments}

We evaluate the capacity of Softmin dynamics to escape local minima and outrun sub-optimal basins by comparing fixed-$\beta$ ($\beta=10$) Softmin against seven baselines: Simulated Annealing (SA) \cite{gelfand1990sampling}; overdamped Langevin dynamics \cite{welling2011bayesian}, a fixed-noise, zero-hyperparameter control; Gradient-Aware Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO) \cite{carrillo2021consensus}, with and without upward $\beta$-annealing; Gradient-Enhanced Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) \cite{noel2012new}; and the canonical, gradient-free formulations of both CBO \cite{CarrilloChoiTotzeckTse2018, pinnau2017consensus} and PSO \cite{kennedy1995particle}. The continuous dynamics are discretized using the Euler-Maruyama scheme, and entry time is measured against a value-space target: a fixed fraction of each benchmark's own depth gap for the wells, or a fixed tolerance above the literature global minimum for Lennard-Jones. Landscape formulations, hyperparameters, initialization bounds, hardware specifications, and real per-particle gradient/objective evaluation counts and wall-clock time are detailed in Appendix \ref{app:hyperparameters}; the source code is available at \cite{softmin_energy_2026}.

\subsection{Lennard-Jones cluster benchmark}
\label{sec:lj-benchmark}

Lennard-Jones clusters are our benchmark for scaling with dimension: $N$ atoms interact through the pairwise potential $V(r)=4\epsilon\bigl[(\sigma/r)^{12}-(\sigma/r)^6\bigr]$ \cite{jones1924determination}, giving a search space of dimension $d=3N$ whose number of local minima grows exponentially in $N$. We use the $N\in\{13,38,55,75\}$ clusters ($d$ up to $225$) from the Cambridge Cluster Database \cite{wales1997global}, whose lowest known energies serve as the literature global minimum against which every method is scored. Following the standard basin-hopping convention of separating global search from local relaxation \cite{wales1997global}, each method's final configuration is locally polished, or \emph{quenched}, via L-BFGS-B to its nearest local minimum before scoring; we report this quenched configuration's energy gap above the literature global minimum, rather than the raw configuration's, so the comparison reflects basin-finding rather than residual local-convergence noise. The strict hit-time target used for the double-well and quadruple-well benchmarks above is rarely reached by any method (Softmin included) beyond the smallest cluster size, once $N\geq38$, so the quenched-energy gap is the informative comparison at this scale.

Softmin's advantage over every baseline \emph{widens}, not narrows, as the cluster grows: the opposite of the ordinary curse-of-dimensionality intuition.

\section{Conclusion}

In this work, we introduced a novel method for escaping local minima in optimization problems, based on the Softmin Energy interaction dynamics. The running best objective value across the ensemble is always nonincreasing, and, whenever $f$ satisfies a Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition on the initial sublevel set, converges to the global minimum at least as fast as ordinary gradient flow (Theorem \ref{thm:ensemble-min}), for any inverse-temperature schedule: the Softmin coupling never slows down the leading particle. Separately, at fixed temperature, the interaction caps the Freidlin--Wentzell quasipotential barrier between adjacent basins at a level of order $1/\beta$, independently of the true barrier height (Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time}). Numerical experiments validate both results. On the double-well and quadruple-well benchmarks, Softmin is the only method that keeps reaching the target as the barrier grows, outperforming Simulated Annealing, Langevin dynamics, Gradient-Enhanced Particle Swarm Optimization, and Gradient-Aware Consensus-Based Optimization in reliability, even where some baselines remain faster on the easiest settings. On the Lennard-Jones cluster benchmark, Softmin's quenched-energy gap above the literature global minimum stays small and essentially flat as the cluster size grows, while every baseline's gap grows roughly linearly with it.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Works}
Our theoretical analysis provides rigorous guarantees in the practically relevant small-noise regime where the Freidlin--Wentzell Large Deviation Principles apply. While the bound in Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time} is not sharp -- in particular $u_c$ and $u_h$ are only upper-estimated by an explicit channel, not computed exactly for general $f$ -- a tighter characterization of the true quasipotential, together with intermediate-noise asymptotics, would sharpen the picture. Our equilibrium characterization (Theorem \ref{thm:stationary-points}) requires no convexity assumption on $f$ whatsoever; the ensemble-descent estimate (Theorem \ref{thm:ensemble-min}) guarantees the running minimum is nonincreasing regardless, but its exponential rate of convergence specifically to the global minimum requires a Polyak–\L{}ojasiewicz condition on the relevant sublevel set, as discussed after its statement. The exit-time consequence of Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time} holds unconditionally only at the level of the quasipotential, and becomes an actual statement about $\E\tau^\sigma$ only under the unverified metastable-domain hypotheses of Corollary \ref{cor:exit-time-conditional}; identifying the exact transition exponent between adjacent basins, and a rigorous large-deviation treatment of the annealed schedule $\beta_t$, remain open.

Future work should address several important directions. First, extending convergence proofs and explicit convergence rates to the non-convex regime, particularly for objectives with multiple disconnected basins, would significantly broaden applicability. Second, analyzing the mean-field limit as $n \to \infty$ could provide insights into the scaling of the method with ensemble size and potentially yield dimension-free convergence rates. Third, \textcolor{purple}{although Theorem \ref{thm:ensemble-min} guarantees the ensemble-descent bound for an arbitrary schedule $\beta_t$,} our experiments (Remark \ref{rem:higher-dim}) found that neither a decaying nor an increasing schedule improved on a well-chosen fixed $\beta$ on the benchmarks considered, contrary to the original motivation for annealing $\beta$ over time; understanding whether this is a property of these specific landscapes or a more general phenomenon, and whether an adaptive schedule driven by the ensemble's own state (rather than a fixed function of $t$) can do better, is an open, concrete direction. Finally, tighter estimates for the exponential rate in the small noise regime can sharpen the theoretical bound to match experimental evidence more closely.

\section{Freidlin--Wentzell Large Deviation Theory}
\label{apx:fw-theory}

This appendix summarizes the background large deviation theory used by Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time} and Corollary \ref{cor:exit-time-conditional}. Consider a small-noise gradient diffusion on $\R^N$,
\[
    \dd\XX_t = -\nabla U(\XX_t)\dd t + \sqrt{2\sigma}\dd B_t,
\]
for a potential $U\in C^2(\R^N)$ and standard $N$-dimensional Brownian motion $(B_t)_{t\ge0}$; in our application $N=nd$ and $U=nJ_\beta$. As $\sigma\downarrow0$, sample paths of this diffusion concentrate around trajectories of the noiseless flow $\dot\XX_t=-\nabla U(\XX_t)$, and rare deviations from this flow are governed by the Freidlin--Wentzell large deviation principle \cite{freidlin2012random}. For an absolutely continuous path $\phi:[0,T]\to\R^N$, the Freidlin--Wentzell action is
\begin{equation}
    I_{0T}(\phi):=\frac14\int_0^T\bigl\|\dot\phi(t)+\nabla U(\phi(t))\bigr\|^2\dd t,
    \label{eq:fw-action}
\end{equation}
and the quasipotential between a point $x\in\R^N$ and a closed set $A\subset\R^N$ is
\begin{equation}
    V(x,A):=\inf_{T>0}\ \inf_{\substack{\phi(0)=x\\ \phi(T)\in A}}I_{0T}(\phi).
    \label{eq:fw-quasipotential}
\end{equation}
Intuitively, $I_{0T}(\phi)$ penalizes deviation from the noiseless flow, vanishing exactly along trajectories of $\dot\phi=-\nabla U(\phi)$; $V(x,A)$ is the cheapest such penalty over all paths from $x$ reaching $A$, and quantifies how atypical (large-deviation-improbable) it is for the diffusion to travel from $x$ to $A$ without the help of noise. The infimum in \eqref{eq:fw-quasipotential} need not be attained by any single finite-time path: it is generally only approximated arbitrarily well, e.g.\ by following the time-reversed noiseless flow toward $x$ and then the forward flow toward $A$, taking arbitrarily long to approach the relevant critical points. This is the sense in which quasipotential values are computed exactly, rather than merely bounded, in the proof of Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time} below.

Under the standard hypotheses of Freidlin--Wentzell exit theory \cite{freidlin2012random} -- in particular that $D$ is a bounded domain with $x\in D$, that $x$ is the unique asymptotically stable equilibrium of the noiseless flow in $D$, and that $\partial D$ contains no other equilibria -- the exit time $\tau_D^\sigma$ of the diffusion from $D$, started at $x$, satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \lim_{\sigma\downarrow0}\sigma\log \E_x\tau_D^\sigma = V(x,\partial D).
    \label{eq:fw-exit-theorem}
\end{equation}

For a gradient drift, the action of any path admits the exact decomposition
\begin{equation}
    I_{0T}(\phi) = U(\phi(T))-U(\phi(0)) + \frac14\int_0^T\bigl\|\dot\phi(t)-\nabla U(\phi(t))\bigr\|^2\dd t,
    \label{eq:gradient-decomposition}
\end{equation}
obtained by expanding the square in \eqref{eq:fw-action} and completing it against $\dd U(\phi(t))/\dd t = \langle\nabla U(\phi(t)),\dot\phi(t)\rangle$. Consequently, any segment of $\phi$ that follows the noiseless flow \emph{downhill} (i.e.\ $\dot\phi=-\nabla U(\phi)$) contributes zero action, while a segment moving \emph{uphill} against the flow (i.e.\ $\dot\phi=\nabla U(\phi)$) costs exactly the potential increase along that segment. This identity is what allows the quasipotential of an explicit admissible path to be computed exactly, rather than merely bounded, in the proof of Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time}.

\subsection{Proofs of Section \ref{sec:gradient-flow-dynamics}}

\begin{lemma}[Component gradient and Hessian at critical configurations]
    \label{lem:derivative-of-J-proof}
    Let $w_k = e^{-\beta f(x_k)}(\sum_j e^{-\beta f(x_j)})^{-1}$ denote the Gibbs weight of particle $x_k$, and let $\prefactor{k}{\xx} = nw_k\left[1 - \beta(f(x_k) - J_\beta(\xx))\right]$ be the dynamic pre-factor. Then
    \begin{equation}
        \nabla_{x_k} (nJ_\beta(\xx)) = \prefactor{k}{\xx} \nabla f(x_k),
        \qquad
        \sum_{k=1}^n \prefactor{k}{\xx} = n.
        \label{eq:lemma-gradient}
    \end{equation}
    Moreover, for $k \neq \ell$ the mixed Hessian block is the outer product
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\partial^2 (nJ_\beta(\xx))}{\partial x_\ell \partial x_k} = \nabla f(x_k)\bigl(\nabla_{x_\ell}\prefactor{k}{\xx}\bigr)^\top,
        \label{eq:mixed-block}
    \end{equation}
    which vanishes whenever $\nabla f(x_k)=0$. Consequently, if $\xx$ is such that $\nabla f(x_k)=0$ for every $k$, then $\nabla(nJ_\beta(\xx))=0$ and the Hessian is block diagonal,
    \begin{equation}
        \nabla^2(nJ_\beta(\xx)) = \operatorname{blockdiag}\bigl(\prefactor{1}{\xx}\nabla^2 f(x_1),\dots,\prefactor{n}{\xx}\nabla^2 f(x_n)\bigr).
        \label{eq:block-diagonal-hessian}
    \end{equation}
    Finally, if $\prefactor{k}{\xx}=0$ then $\frac{\partial^2(nJ_\beta(\xx))}{\partial x_k^2} = -n\beta w_k \nabla f(x_k)\nabla f(x_k)^\top$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
    We compute the derivatives using the chain rule and the properties of the Gibbs weights. First, we establish the gradient of the weight $w_i$ with respect to $x_k$:
    \begin{equation*}
        \nabla_{x_k} w_i = \begin{cases}
            \beta w_i w_k \nabla f(x_k) & \text{if } i \neq k, \\
            -\beta w_k (1 - w_k) \nabla f(x_k) & \text{if } i = k.
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation*}
    For the first derivative of $nJ_\beta(\xx) = n\sum_i w_i f(x_i)$, an identical computation to the main text gives $\nabla_{x_k}(nJ_\beta(\xx)) = nw_k[1-\beta(f(x_k)-J_\beta(\xx))]\nabla f(x_k) = \prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla f(x_k)$; summing over $k$ and using $\sum_k w_k=1$, $\sum_k w_kf(x_k)=J_\beta(\xx)$, gives $\sum_k\prefactor{k}{\xx}=n[1-\beta(J_\beta(\xx)-J_\beta(\xx))]=n$.

    For the mixed second derivative ($k\ne\ell$), we differentiate $\nabla_{x_k}(nJ_\beta(\xx))=\prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla f(x_k)$ with respect to $x_\ell$. Because $\nabla f(x_k)$ is independent of $x_\ell$, the Jacobian matrix is the outer product $\nabla f(x_k)(\nabla_{x_\ell}\prefactor{k}{\xx})^\top$. Substituting $\nabla_{x_\ell}w_k=\beta w_kw_\ell\nabla f(x_\ell)$ and $\nabla_{x_\ell}J_\beta(\xx)=\frac1n\prefactor{\ell}{\xx}\nabla f(x_\ell)$,
    \begin{align*}
        \nabla_{x_\ell}\prefactor{k}{\xx} &= n(\nabla_{x_\ell}w_k)\left[1-\beta(f(x_k)-J_\beta(\xx))\right] + nw_k\beta\left(\nabla_{x_\ell}J_\beta(\xx)\right) \\
        &= \beta w_\ell\bigl(nw_k[1-\beta(f(x_k)-J_\beta(\xx))]\bigr)\nabla f(x_\ell) + \beta w_k\prefactor{\ell}{\xx}\nabla f(x_\ell) \\
        &= \beta\left[w_\ell\prefactor{k}{\xx}+w_k\prefactor{\ell}{\xx}\right]\nabla f(x_\ell),
    \end{align*}
    which gives \eqref{eq:mixed-block} and vanishes whenever $\nabla f(x_k)=0$ (or symmetrically $\nabla f(x_\ell)=0$). If $\nabla f(x_k)=0$ for every $k$, every off-diagonal block vanishes and \eqref{eq:lemma-gradient} gives $\nabla(nJ_\beta(\xx))=0$; the diagonal block computed below then reduces to $\prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla^2f(x_k)$, giving the block-diagonal form \eqref{eq:block-diagonal-hessian}.

    Finally, for the diagonal block ($k=\ell$), applying the product rule to $\nabla_{x_k}(nJ_\beta(\xx))=\prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla f(x_k)$ includes the local Hessian $\nabla^2 f(x_k)$:
    \[
        \frac{\partial^2(nJ_\beta(\xx))}{\partial x_k^2} = \prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla^2f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)\bigl(\nabla_{x_k}\prefactor{k}{\xx}\bigr)^\top.
    \]
    Using $\nabla_{x_k}w_k=-\beta w_k(1-w_k)\nabla f(x_k)$ and $\nabla_{x_k}J_\beta(\xx)=\frac1n\prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla f(x_k)$, a computation identical in structure to the mixed case gives
    \[
        \nabla_{x_k}\prefactor{k}{\xx} = -\beta\left[nw_k+\prefactor{k}{\xx}-2w_k\prefactor{k}{\xx}\right]\nabla f(x_k),
    \]
    so that, substituting back,
    \[
        \frac{\partial^2(nJ_\beta(\xx))}{\partial x_k^2} = \prefactor{k}{\xx}\nabla^2f(x_k) - \beta\left[nw_k+\prefactor{k}{\xx}-2w_k\prefactor{k}{\xx}\right]\nabla f(x_k)\nabla f(x_k)^\top,
    \]
    which reduces to $-n\beta w_k\nabla f(x_k)\nabla f(x_k)^\top$ when $\prefactor{k}{\xx}=0$. \qedhere
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem \ref{thm:stationary-points}]
\label{thm:stationary-points-proof}
    A locally asymptotically stable equilibrium of the gradient flow \eqref{eq:gradient-flow} is an isolated local minimum of $nJ_\beta$: indeed $\frac{d}{dt}(nJ_\beta(\xx(t)))=-\|\nabla(nJ_\beta)\|^2\le0$, so if points arbitrarily close to $\bar\xx$ had strictly smaller value the trajectories starting there could not converge to $\bar\xx$; and a non-isolated equilibrium admits nearby constant trajectories that likewise do not converge to $\bar\xx$.

    Fix $k$ and freeze the other particles at $\bar x_j$, $j\ne k$. Since $nJ_\beta$ depends on $x_k$ only through $r=f(x_k)$, we may write
    \[
        nJ_\beta(\bar x_1,\dots,x_k,\dots,\bar x_n)=g(f(x_k)), \qquad g(r):=n\,\frac{re^{-\beta r}+C_1}{e^{-\beta r}+C_0},
    \]
    with $C_0:=\sum_{j\ne k}e^{-\beta f(\bar x_j)}>0$ and $C_1:=\sum_{j\ne k}f(\bar x_j)e^{-\beta f(\bar x_j)}$. By the chain rule and Lemma \ref{lem:derivative-of-J-proof}, wherever $\nabla f(x_k)\ne0$ one has $g'(f(x_k))=\prefactor{k}{\xx}$; by continuity of both sides in $f(x_k)$, $g'(f(\bar x_k))=\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}$. Writing $g'(r)=n\omega(r)[1-\beta(r-g(r)/n)]$ with $\omega(r)=e^{-\beta r}/(e^{-\beta r}+C_0)\in(0,1)$, a direct computation shows that at any critical point $r_0$ of $g$ (where the bracket vanishes), $g''(r_0)=-n\beta\omega(r_0)<0$.

    \emph{Step 1: $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}\ne0$.} Suppose $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}=0$. By the above, $r_0=f(\bar x_k)$ is a critical point of $g$, and it is a strict local maximum. If $f$ is non-constant near $\bar x_k$, there are points $y$ arbitrarily close to $\bar x_k$ with $f(y)$ close to but different from $r_0$, whence $nJ_\beta(\dots,y,\dots)=g(f(y))<g(r_0)=nJ_\beta(\bar\xx)$, contradicting local minimality. If $f$ is constant near $\bar x_k$, moving $x_k$ within that level set leaves every objective value, Gibbs weight, and gradient unchanged, producing a continuum of equilibria arbitrarily close to $\bar\xx$ and contradicting isolation. Hence $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}\ne0$.

    \emph{Step 2: $\nabla f(\bar x_k)=0$.} Since $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}\ne0$, $g'(f(\bar x_k))\ne0$, so $g$ is strictly monotone near $f(\bar x_k)$. If $\nabla f(\bar x_k)\ne0$, then $f$ takes values on both sides of $f(\bar x_k)$ arbitrarily near $\bar x_k$; strict monotonicity of $g$ then yields nearby configurations with $nJ_\beta<nJ_\beta(\bar\xx)$, contradicting local minimality. Hence $\nabla f(\bar x_k)=0$.

    \emph{Step 3: classification.} If $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}>0$, then $g$ is strictly increasing near $f(\bar x_k)$, so local minimality of $nJ_\beta$ forces $f(x_k)\ge f(\bar x_k)$ for $x_k$ near $\bar x_k$; thus $\bar x_k$ is a local minimum of $f$. If $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}<0$, the symmetric argument shows $\bar x_k$ is a local maximum. Because $nw_k>0$, the sign of $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}$ is that of $1-\beta(f(\bar x_k)-J_\beta(\bar\xx))$, giving the stated threshold. Finally, $\sum_k\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}=n$ forces some $\prefactor{k}{\bar\xx}>0$, so at least one particle is at a local minimum of $f$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem \ref{thm:ensemble-min}]
\label{thm:ensemble-min-proof}
    Fix $t$ and let $i$ attain the minimum, $f(x_i(t))=m(t)$. Since $f(x_i(t))\le f(x_j(t))$ for all $j$, we have $e^{-\beta_tf(x_j(t))}\le e^{-\beta_tf(x_i(t))}$, hence $\sum_je^{-\beta_tf(x_j(t))}\le ne^{-\beta_tf(x_i(t))}$ and therefore $nw_i(t)\ge1$. As $J_{\beta_t}(\xx(t))$ is a convex combination of the values $f(x_j(t))$, it satisfies $J_{\beta_t}(\xx(t))\ge f(x_i(t))$, so $1-\beta_t(f(x_i(t))-J_{\beta_t}(\xx(t)))\ge1$. Multiplying,
    \begin{equation}
        \prefactor{i}{\xx(t)}\ge1 \qquad \text{for every } i \text{ attaining the minimum at } t.
        \label{eq:ai-ge-1}
    \end{equation}
    Along such a particle,
    \[
        \frac{d}{dt}f(x_i(t)) = \langle\nabla f(x_i(t)),\dot x_i(t)\rangle = -\prefactor{i}{\xx(t)}\|\nabla f(x_i(t))\|^2 \le -\|\nabla f(x_i(t))\|^2.
    \]

    \textcolor{purple}{Consider $m=\min_k g_k$ with $g_k(t)=f(x_k(t))$. Since $\beta_t$ is only assumed measurable, each $x_k$ is a Carath\'eodory solution and hence absolutely continuous; as $f$ has locally Lipschitz gradient, each $g_k$ is absolutely continuous on compact time intervals, and a pointwise minimum of finitely many absolutely continuous functions is absolutely continuous. Therefore $m$ is absolutely continuous, and $m$ together with every $g_k$ is differentiable at a.e.\ $t$. At any such $t$, choose $i$ attaining the minimum; the function $g_i-m\ge0$ is then differentiable at $t$ and vanishes there, so it has zero derivative at $t$, giving $m'(t)=g_i'(t)$.} Combined with the previous display,
    \[
        m'(t)=-\prefactor{i}{\xx(t)}\|\nabla f(x_i(t))\|^2\le-\|\nabla f(x_i(t))\|^2\le0 \qquad \text{for a.e. } t.
    \]
    Integrating, $m$ is nonincreasing, so every minimizing particle stays in $S_0=\{x:f(x)\le m(0)\}$, where the Polyak--\L{}ojasiewicz condition (Definition \ref{def:pl-condition}) applies:
    \[
        m'(t)\le-\|\nabla f(x_i(t))\|^2\le-2\mu\bigl(f(x_i(t))-f_\star\bigr)=-2\mu\bigl(m(t)-f_\star\bigr).
    \]
    Setting $y(t):=e^{2\mu t}(m(t)-f_\star)$, which is absolutely continuous, we get $y'(t)=e^{2\mu t}[m'(t)+2\mu(m(t)-f_\star)]\le0$ for a.e.\ $t$, so $y(t)\le y(0)$, i.e.
    \[
        m(t)-f_\star\le e^{-2\mu t}\bigl(m(0)-f_\star\bigr), \qquad t\ge0. \qedhere
    \]
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proofs of Section \ref{sec:hitting-times}}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma \ref{lem:channel-drift}]
\label{lem:channel-drift-proof}
    Write $r:=r(x)$. Factoring $e^{-\beta f(z_-)}$ out of the Gibbs weights at $\Gamma(x)$,
    \[
        w_1(\Gamma(x))=\frac{e^{-\beta r}}{n-1+e^{-\beta r}}, \qquad w_j(\Gamma(x))=\frac{1}{n-1+e^{-\beta r}} \quad (j\ge2),
    \]
    whence $J_\beta(\Gamma(x))-f(z_-)=re^{-\beta r}/(n-1+e^{-\beta r})=u(r)/n$, which is \eqref{eq:reduced-potential}. Therefore
    \[
        f(x)-J_\beta(\Gamma(x)) = r-\frac{re^{-\beta r}}{n-1+e^{-\beta r}} = \frac{(n-1)r}{n-1+e^{-\beta r}},
    \]
    and substituting into $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}=nw_1(\Gamma(x))[1-\beta(f(x)-J_\beta(\Gamma(x)))]$ gives
    \[
        \prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)} = \frac{ne^{-\beta r}}{n-1+e^{-\beta r}}\cdot\frac{n-1+e^{-\beta r}-\beta(n-1)r}{n-1+e^{-\beta r}} = \frac{ne^{-\beta r}q(r)}{(n-1+e^{-\beta r})^2}.
    \]
    Differentiating \eqref{eq:reduced-potential} directly yields the same expression for $u'(r)$, proving \eqref{eq:a1-equals-uprime}, consistently with the identity $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}=u'(r(x))$ already established in Lemma \ref{lem:channel-invariant}. In particular $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}=0$ if and only if $q(r)=0$, which by the displayed formula for $f(x)-J_\beta$ is equivalent to $f(x)-J_\beta(\Gamma(x))=1/\beta$, i.e.\ to the vanishing of the Softmin threshold gap of the active particle.

    Since $q'(r)=-\beta(n-1)-\beta e^{-\beta r}<0$ for all $r\in\R$, $q(0)=n>0$, and $q(r)\to-\infty$ as $r\to\infty$, $q$ has a unique zero $r_c>0$. From $q(1/\beta)=e^{-1}>0$ we get $r_c>1/\beta$; and $q(r_c)=0$ reads $(n-1)(\beta r_c-1)=e^{-\beta r_c}<1$, giving $\beta r_c<n/(n-1)$. This is \eqref{eq:rc-location}. As $ne^{-\beta r}/(n-1+e^{-\beta r})^2>0$, the sign of $u'$ is the sign of $q$, so $u$ is strictly increasing on $(-\infty,r_c]$ and strictly decreasing on $[r_c,\infty)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time}]
\label{thm:transition-time-proof}
    \emph{Part 1.} For $r\le0$ we have $u(r)\le0$. For $r>0$, since $n-1+e^{-\beta r}>n-1$,
    \[
        u(r)<\frac{n}{n-1}re^{-\beta r}\le\frac{n}{n-1}\max_{\rho\ge0}\rho e^{-\beta\rho}=\frac{n}{e(n-1)\beta},
    \]
    the maximum being attained at $\rho=1/\beta$. Hence $u_c=\max_\R u<n/(e(n-1)\beta)$, which is \eqref{eq:barrier-cap}; the bound on $r_c$ is \eqref{eq:rc-location}.

    \emph{Part 2.} A channel path has the form $\phi(t)=\Gamma(\psi(t))$; the frozen coordinates stay at the critical point $z_-$, so their drift vanishes and, with $W(x):=u(r(x))$, the action reduces to $I_{0T}=\frac14\int_0^T|\dot\psi+W'(\psi)|^2\dd t$. The one-dimensional version of \eqref{eq:gradient-decomposition} is
    \[
        I_{0T}=W(\psi(T))-W(\psi(0))+\frac14\int_0^T|\dot\psi-W'(\psi)|^2\dd t.
    \]
    By \eqref{eq:adjacent-well-monotonicity}, $r$ is a strictly increasing bijection from $[z_-,s]$ onto $[0,h]$, so there is a unique $x_m\in(z_-,s]$ with $r(x_m)=\min\{h,r_c\}$. On $[z_-,x_m]$ we have $r(x)\le r_c$, so by Lemma \ref{lem:channel-drift} $W$ is strictly increasing there, with total rise $W(x_m)-W(z_-)=u_h$; on $[x_m,s]$ we have $r(x)\ge r_c$, so $W$ is nonincreasing. Every continuous channel path from $z_-$ to $s$ passes through $x_m$, and applying the displayed decomposition to the sub-path up to the first hitting time of $x_m$ bounds the action from below by $u_h$. This value is attained in the quasipotential sense by following the reversed deterministic flow from $z_-$ up to $x_m$ (cost exactly $u_h$) and then the forward flow toward $s$ at zero additional cost, with the standard finite-time approximations near the critical points. Therefore the channel quasipotential equals $u_h$, and since $V_\beta$ is an infimum over the larger class of all paths in $\R^n$,
    \[
        V_\beta(\Gamma(z_-),\Gamma(s))\le u_h.
    \]
    Because $u(r)/r=ne^{-\beta r}/(n-1+e^{-\beta r})<1$ for $r>0$, we have $u_h<\min\{h,r_c\}\le h$, which gives \eqref{eq:initial-bound}.

    \emph{Part 3.} Assume $r_c<h$. At $\Gamma(s)$ every particle sits at a critical point of $f$ (either $s$ or $z_-$). For the active particle, \eqref{eq:a1-equals-uprime} gives $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(s)}=u'(h)<0$ because $h>r_c$, and $f''(s)<0$ since $s$ is a nondegenerate maximum. For a frozen particle at $z_-$,
    \[
        \prefactor{j}{\Gamma(s)} = nw_j(\Gamma(s))\bigl[1+\beta\bigl(J_\beta(\Gamma(s))-f(z_-)\bigr)\bigr]>0
    \]
    by \eqref{eq:reduced-potential}, since $J_\beta(\Gamma(s))-f(z_-)=u(h)/n>0$, and $f''(z_-)>0$. Hence Corollary \ref{cor:stability-sufficient} applies and $\Gamma(s)$ is a strict local minimum of $nJ_\beta$. On the $z_-$ side, $r$ decreases strictly from $h$ through $r_c$ as $x$ moves from $s$ toward $z_-$ (recall $x_m\in(z_-,s)$ is where $r(x_m)=r_c$, well-defined since $r_c<h$), so the channel well at $\Gamma(s)$ has barrier $u_c-u(h)$ on this side unconditionally --- this uses only $r_c<h$, not $\rho_+$.

    The $z_+$ side depends on $\rho_+$. On $(s,z_+)$, $r$ decreases strictly from $h$ to $\rho_+$ by \eqref{eq:adjacent-well-monotonicity}--\eqref{eq:relative-energy}.

    If $\rho_+<r_c$, then $r$ crosses $r_c$ on this interval, so there is a unique $\xi_+$ with $r(\xi_+)=r_c$. Between $s$ and $\xi_+$ the reduced potential rises from $u(h)$ to $u_c$; the gradient-action argument of Part 2 gives channel action $u_c-u(h)$, and since $0<u(h)<u_c$ we obtain \eqref{eq:post-saddle-bound}, so the well has matching barrier heights on both sides. Finally, for $x\in(\xi_+,z_+)$ we have $r(x)<r_c$, hence $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}=u'(r(x))>0$ by \eqref{eq:a1-equals-uprime}, while $f'(x)<0$; therefore the active coordinate of the deterministic channel flow satisfies $\dot x=-\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(x)}f'(x)>0$, driving the particle toward $z_+$. Concatenating the admissible path of Part 2 (from $\Gamma(z_-)$ to $\Gamma(s)$, action $u_h$) with this one (from $\Gamma(s)$ to $\Gamma(\xi_+)$, action $u_c-u(h)$) gives an admissible path from $\Gamma(z_-)$ to $\Gamma(\xi_+)$ of total action $u_h+(u_c-u(h))$, proving \eqref{eq:two-stage-bound}.

    If instead $\rho_+\ge r_c$, then $r(x)\in[r_c,h)$ for every $x\in(s,z_+)$: the interval lies entirely on the decreasing side of $u$, where \textcolor{purple}{$u$ is strictly decreasing in $r$ while $r$ is strictly decreasing in $x$}, so $u(r(x))$ is strictly increasing in $x$ throughout $(s,z_+)$, from $u(h)$ to $u(\rho_+)$. No interior peak occurs, so no point $\xi_+$ with $r(\xi_+)=r_c$ exists in $(s,z_+)$\textcolor{purple}{: the channel potential rises monotonically to $\Gamma(z_+)$ with no intermediate barrier to cross, and the total rise $u(\rho_+)-u(h)$ is at most the $z_-$-side barrier $u_c-u(h)$, since $u(\rho_+)\le u_c$. In this regime $\prefactor{1}{\Gamma(z_+)}=u'(\rho_+)\le0$ while $f''(z_+)>0$, so by Theorem \ref{thm:stationary-points} $\Gamma(z_+)$ is not itself a stable equilibrium, and no post-saddle well forms on this side.}

    If instead $h\le r_c$, then $r(x)\le h\le r_c$ for $x$ near $s$, with equality only at $x=s$, and the strict monotonicity of $u$ on $(-\infty,r_c]$ shows that $x\mapsto u(r(x))$ has a strict local maximum at $s$. Hence $\Gamma(s)$ is not a local minimum of $nJ_\beta$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{General Experimental Settings}
Table \ref{tab:general_settings} summarizes the shared parameters utilized across all algorithms for each specific landscape. The swarm size and maximum step limits were kept constant to provide a fair baseline for evaluating the topological escape mechanisms. Domain bounds for the 1D and 2D wells are derived directly from each landscape's own critical points rather than set by hand: the domain extends $K=5$ natural harmonic fluctuation-lengthscales ($1/\sqrt{f''}$ at the relevant minimum, or the softest Hessian eigendirection in 2D) beyond the outermost minimum -- the same role a $5\sigma$ truncation plays for a locally-Gaussian process, giving negligible boundary-contamination probability without an oversized domain. A fixed absolute margin was checked and found to represent anywhere from $\sim\!1$ to $\sim\!5.5$ lengthscales across the swept difficulty range, an uncalibrated $>\!5\times$ inconsistency this formula removes. The quadruple well is swept over $a\in\{2,\dots,30\}$ (not $1$): at $a=1$ the softest Hessian eigenvalue at the well vanishes exactly, so the landscape does not yet have a genuine local minimum in that direction.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Selection Methodology}
\label{app:hyperparameter-methodology}

Each of the 100 independent runs (Table \ref{tab:general_settings}) is indexed by an integer $\text{seed}\in\{0,\dots,99\}$, and every worker seeds NumPy's global RNG once via \texttt{np.random.seed(seed*1000)} before drawing that run's initial swarm and simulating every algorithm on it. This seed indexing is reused identically at every sweep point (every $\delta$, $A$, or $d$) rather than redrawn independently per point: \texttt{seed=7} at $\delta=5$ and \texttt{seed=7} at $\delta=25$ consume the same underlying draw from the same seeded RNG state, remapped only by that point's own initialization bounds. Consequently the 100-run samples at different sweep points are correlated, not independent, and the quartile error bars in Figure \ref{fig:experiments} should be interpreted as within-seed variability across the ensemble rather than as independent per-point confidence intervals.

Learning rates are unified to $\eta=0.001$ across every benchmark and algorithm (the Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time} verification drivers excepted, which use their own, separately-motivated small-noise/small-step-size regime). The Softmin inverse temperature $\beta$ is selected per benchmark family via a two-stage, code-audited procedure (\texttt{src/hyperparameter\_diagnostics.py}), evaluated at a representative \emph{middle}-of-sweep setting rather than the hardest one, so the choice is not fit to the one condition the sweep is meant to stress-test: (1) an initial screen measures the distribution of $\beta(f_i-J_\beta)$ across the ensemble, flagging candidate values where trailing particles are not uniformly driven into the exponentially-suppressed regime; (2) because that screen alone favors values that are too small -- it only measures whether every particle receives rescue-drift, not what the reported first-entry-time metric rewards, namely the single fastest particle reaching the target, which benefits from a \emph{concentrated} ensemble via the leading particle's prefactor approaching $n$ -- the candidates are then validated directly by measuring actual entry time. Across every benchmark tested (1D double well, 2D quadruple well, high-dimensional Ackley, and a spot check on Lennard-Jones), this second step confirmed $\beta=10$ as at or near the empirical optimum, matching the value used throughout; going lower is consistently slower or fails to reach the target at all, and going higher does not help (and is markedly worse on the quadruple well). The same direct-validation procedure was applied to Simulated Annealing's geometric cooling rate, and to comparing $\beta$ held fixed against both a decaying and an increasing schedule -- see Remark \ref{rem:higher-dim} for the latter result. The Lennard-Jones benchmark additionally clips the gradient's Lennard-Jones term (never the confinement term, whose restoring force must scale freely to remain able to pull back an atom that has drifted outside the container) to a per-atom norm of 1.0 before the swarm's discrete update, applied identically to every algorithm; this clip is not applied to the L-BFGS-B local polish (``quench'') of each method's final configuration, which uses the true, unclipped gradient throughout, since a quasi-Newton method's curvature estimate is only valid for the true gradient.

\paragraph{Baselines received the same budget, not literature defaults asserted without checking.} Simulated Annealing, CBO, and PSO were given the identical diagnostic-guided procedure and, where a comparable parameterization exists, the identical candidate budget as Softmin's $\beta$ (Table \ref{tab:calibration_budget}), rather than unexamined literature-standard values. \emph{Simulated Annealing}: seven candidate cooling rates were derived from target cooling \emph{timescales} $t^\star\in\{10^3,3{\times}10^3,10^4,3{\times}10^4,10^5,3{\times}10^5,10^6\}$ (steps to reach 5\% of the initial temperature, $\text{rate}=0.05^{1/t^\star}$) rather than hand-picked decimals, and validated directly the same way as $\beta$; the shipped rate ($0.99995$) was reconfirmed on both wells. SA's initial temperature $T_0$ (previously an unexamined a priori default of $10$ -- not a literature-sourced value; $T_0$ is inherently problem-scale-dependent, so no universal literature default exists) was calibrated separately, via a Kramers/Arrhenius criterion rather than escape time (minimizing escape time is degenerate for $T_0$ in this continuous, always-accept diffusion: larger $T_0$ is monotonically faster with no interior optimum, eventually indistinguishable from bounded random search): five candidates $T_0=f\cdot h$, $f\in\{0.25,0.5,1,2,4\}$, where $h$ is the double well's mid-sweep barrier height, were scored by the closed-form escape factor $\exp(-h/T_0)$ landing in a \emph{low} target band $[0.01,0.05]$ -- a genuine, non-trivial barrier, not the near-unity band a literal reading of the discrete-Metropolis initial-acceptance-ratio heuristic (Kirkpatrick et al., 1983) would suggest, which would leave the barrier already thermodynamically negligible from $t=0$ and trivialize exactly the obstacle this baseline is meant to face. $T_0=h/4$ was selected and validated directly, reaching the target within a few hundred steps at the calibration midpoint and transferring to the quadruple well; like CBO and PSO below, it fails at the hardest points of the double-well sweep ($\delta\gtrsim 25$), reported as a genuine negative result rather than papered over with a per-$\delta$ schedule -- independently re-confirmed (2026-08-07) with early-stopping disabled entirely and a 1{,}500{,}000-step horizon (15$\times$ the original probe): \texttt{best\_global\_value} is bit-for-bit identical from step 10{,}000 onward at both $\delta=25$ and $\delta=30$, consistent with SA's own geometric cooling schedule driving the temperature to $\approx 0$ well before step 200{,}000 and permanently freezing the dynamics, not a premature-abort artifact. The same criterion applied to Ackley's own barrier scale was inconclusive even at a five-times-longer diagnostic budget, so Ackley and Lennard-Jones retain $T_0=10$. \emph{CBO}: the same 11-value $\beta$ grid used for Softmin was swept, together with two annealed-schedule variants (growth-only, unlike Softmin's decaying schedule, matching CBO's own literature convention). The result is a genuine negative finding, not an oversight: every candidate times out via consensus collapse on the double well, the quadruple well, \emph{and} Ackley, at the identical step \emph{regardless of which $\beta$ was used} -- direct testing across $\beta\in\{0.01,0.02,0.1,1.0,10.0\}$ found position\_collapse at the same step on every benchmark tested, confirming the collapse is a $\beta$-independent structural property of vanilla CBO's self-shrinking anisotropic diffusion, not a symptom of a poorly-chosen $\beta$. Since the choice is empirically immaterial, $\beta=0.01$ (CBO's literature-typical range; unlike PSO's constriction coefficients below, CBO has no single canonical numeric $\beta$, but 0.01--0.02 is the commonly cited scale) is used in place of the previous unexamined a priori default of 10. \emph{PSO} has no single temperature-like scalar to sweep, so six named, literature-grounded $(w,c_1,c_2)$ triples were tested instead (the canonical Clerc--Kennedy constriction coefficients used throughout; the original Kennedy--Eberhart rule; both endpoints of the Eberhart--Shi linearly-decreasing-inertia schedule; a social-weighted variant biased toward the swarm's global best; and a deliberately non-contractive stress-test triple), pre-filtered by a finite/at-bound stability probe. On the double well and quadruple well, PSO shows the same negative result as CBO. On Ackley, however, the literature-standard default itself \emph{fails} -- timing out via consensus collapse at every dimension tested ($d=50,70,90$) -- while two of the six alternatives succeed at all three; the faster, $(w,c_1,c_2)=(0.4,2.0,2.0)$, was adopted for Ackley and is used for the Ackley-$d{=}50$ ablation in \S\ref{apx:ablation-study}; a spot check found it also improves the Lennard-Jones quenched result, so it is used there too (Table \ref{tab:general_settings}).

\subsubsection{Ablation Study: Ascent-Descent Mechanism}
\label{apx:ablation-study}

We isolate the contribution of the sign-changing prefactor $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$ itself by comparing the real (\emph{signed}) mechanism against two ablations: \emph{positive part}, $(\prefactor{k}{\xx})_+$, which freezes any particle the real mechanism would have pushed uphill instead of letting it ascend; and \emph{absolute value}, $|\prefactor{k}{\xx}|$, which forces every particle to always descend, at whatever magnitude the real mechanism would have applied. All three variants share the same Gibbs/softmax weighting $n\cdot w_k$ that scales $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$ in the first place -- only the sign treatment on top of it differs.

The result is not the clean "signed wins, the ablations degrade" story the sign-flipping mechanism might suggest. On the two swept well benchmarks (Figure \ref{fig:ablation-wells}), there is no consistent winner: at the easier settings signed is competitive or best (e.g. quadruple well $A=15$: signed $0.25$, positive part $0.29$, absolute $0.34$), but at harder settings absolute is clearly faster -- double well $\delta=15$: signed $3.81$, positive part $3.06$, absolute $2.20$; quadruple well $A=30$: signed $32.69$, positive part $30.70$, absolute $10.82$, roughly $3\times$ faster than either alternative that still preserves or freezes the sign. At the two fixed, hard settings (Figure \ref{fig:ablation-fixed}) the three modes are close to indistinguishable: Ackley $d=50$ entry time (mean $100.1$/$98.2$/$99.4$ for signed/positive part/absolute, all 100\% reached) and Lennard-Jones $N=38$ quenched-energy gap ($2.26$/$2.48$/$2.40$) show heavily overlapping distributions.

Taken together, these four benchmarks suggest that preserving the sign of $\prefactor{k}{\xx}$ -- letting trailing particles genuinely ascend rather than merely stall or be redirected downhill -- is not what is doing most of the practical work in this noise regime ($\sigma=1$). The Gibbs/softmax weighting $n\cdot w_k$, identical across all three variants, already strongly suppresses trailing particles' contribution regardless of the sign convention applied on top of it; that shared attenuation is the more plausible source of the empirical advantage over the baselines in Section \ref{sec:numerical-experiments}. The sign-flipping ascent mechanism remains what Theorem \ref{thm:transition-time} is stated and proved for, and is a $\sigma\to 0$ asymptotic statement -- these benchmarks run at a fixed, non-vanishing $\sigma$, and the ablation results here are consistent with the sign convention mattering more as $\sigma$ shrinks than it does at the noise level tested.
